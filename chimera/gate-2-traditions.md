# PROJECT CHIMERA — THE TRADITIONS (Gate 2)

*What Gate 1 asked this gate for, in its own words: "schools, doctrines and traditions of casting — how the trade organises its teaching, and what different traditions do differently with the same two strokes."*

Nothing here amends Gate 1. Coinages spent: zero of ten.

---

## 1. Taught, or Found

Nobody in this world picks a school.

A person with the gift comes to the work the way anyone comes to any work: somebody was standing there, and taught her, and that somebody had been taught by somebody else. Or nobody was standing there, and she needed the thing done, and she worked it out with what she had on her.

That is the whole of it. The five traditions in this document are not five kinds of power. There is one practice — solve, then set, by the rules in Gate 1, at the same three speeds, for everyone. A tradition is a different answer to one question, *where does the substance come from*, and the answer is almost always just whatever was available to the person who taught you.

So the traditions arrange on a line, from taught to found:

**The attuned** are taught before they can remember. **The bag** is apprenticed, formally, with a term and a master. **The instrument** is bought and fitted, and taught by whoever sold it. **The scholar** works out of other people's records first and then by her own hands. **The body** was taught by nobody.

What puts a person at one end rather than the other is not talent and not temperament. It is circumstance: who was there, what the family could pay for, whether the trade would take her, whether there was a rod to inherit, whether anyone in that district still knows how — and, more often than the trade likes to say out loud, what she once had to do in a hurry.

These are tendencies and not walls, and the interesting people live on the line rather than at the ends of it. A woman apprenticed to a bag caster in a district where the supply dried up twenty years ago drifts toward her own skin, because that is what is left. A scholar who inherits her mother's rod is both, and will be asked to choose by people who think it matters. A body caster who is finally taken on by somebody late, at thirty, spends the rest of her life with an apprentice's habits and a map that already stops at her elbow.

## 2. Who Can Do This At All

Almost nobody.

The read is not hereditary, nobody is tested for it and shut out at twelve, and no house owns it. Everybody has some. But the low end of the scale is a slope so steep that the overwhelming majority of people alive will never solve anything, not once, and will never try — not because they are forbidden, but because the climb is not worth what it returns. A person of ordinary acuity who devoted her life to it would end where a gifted fourteen-year-old starts, and everyone can see that, and so almost nobody does it.

The trade knows this and describes it in exactly those terms: acuity, ceiling, years. Ordinary people do not talk that way. Ordinary people say **the gift**, and mean the top of the slope, and are not wrong about anything that matters to them.

Keep both vocabularies. A foreman says *she reads well and she started young.* Her aunt says *she has the gift, God help her.* The gap between those two sentences is one of the most reliable ways to tell, on the page, whose point of view you are in.

Because it is a lottery and not a lineage, the gift turns up anywhere. No family can hold it, nobody has ever bred for it successfully, and every generation the trade goes looking again in places it would rather not. A house with three casters in four generations thinks itself a dynasty and is, statistically, lucky. A gifted child born to a channel gang is not a story about destiny; she is a story about who notices, and whether anyone in that district still knows enough to teach her.

**And the industry does not run on casters.** This is the single most important thing in this section and it is easy to get backwards. Sap is pulled by extractor men working a beam engine over a throat. It is moved by channel gangs and sinkers and gate crews. It is carried by hauliers with flask and cored stone. It is measured on drawing floors. The dead are handled through their nine days by layers-out. Not one of those jobs requires the gift, and there are vastly more people doing them than there are casters in the world.

So the sap economy is not a casters' economy. It is an ordinary industrial economy with a very small number of extremely useful people distributed through it, and those people are expensive, injured young, and not replaceable on any schedule. A works can lose a caster and hire another in a season. It cannot lose its channel gang and do anything at all.

## 3. The Credential Is a Name

Nobody licenses casting.

There is no register, no seal, no inspector, no examination that ends in a certificate. It has been proposed. It has never survived contact with the work: a field surgeon solving bone shards out of a wound has forty seconds and cannot wait on paperwork, and no state has ever found a way to stand between a person and her own two hands.

What the world has instead is a question, and everyone in the trade asks it inside the first minute.

> *Who taught you?*

A name is the credential. Who taught you, and who taught them, and whether that name is one anybody has heard. It gets you hired sight unseen in a town you have never been to. It follows you between towns when it is bad. It is asked at a hiring, at a gate, across a table in a taproom, and by the sister of a man who is about to stand inside your boundary.

The reason it holds — and this is not sentiment, it is arithmetic — is that the trade has a very high rate of injury and everybody knows it. To vouch for someone is to put your own name behind the claim that she says her source out loud, every single time, including when she is tired and it is the ninth hour and the thing is going wrong. That is worth more than any inspection anybody could staff, because the inspector would not be in the room and the master's reputation is.

Three things follow, and they are load-bearing for the rest of this document.

**A master's name outlives the master.** Casters are placed in a line, and the line is spoken aloud: *she trained under him, and he under her.* A bad death in the third generation still costs the first. This is the closest thing the trade has to an institution and it is entirely informal.

**Vouching is refusable, and refusal is the sanction.** There is no licence to revoke. What there is instead is a master who stops answering the question, and a district that notices within a month.

**And it locks out anyone with no name to give.** The body caster taught herself because nobody would take her. When she is asked — and she is always asked — she has no answer. The answer she does not have is written up her arm, where everyone in the room can already see it.

## 4. The Bag

**Casts with carried reagents.** Flint, carbon, salts, filings, and accelerant. Graded, weighed, and paid for.

### How you come to it

Apprenticed, and it is the most formal path in the trade. There is a term, there is a master, and there is a great deal of standing over you.

What an apprentice does for the first year is not casting. It is weighing, grading, and being made to say the line out loud until it is a habit she cannot drop — and then being made to say *which* lime, and *how much*, until "enough" stops being an answer she is allowed to give. A bag master's whole teaching method is refusing to accept a vague source. That is why bag casters, decades later, still name their source aloud in an empty room. It was drilled into them by someone who did not think it was optional.

### How you live

Inside a supply chain, and it is the defining fact of the tradition.

Somebody mills her stock. Somebody grades it. Somebody, sooner or later, cuts it. She has a supplier she trusts and a supplier she uses when the first one is out, and she can tell you the difference in one sentence. She draws a bead in the shop before she buys, and watches how far the frost climbs, like everyone else who handles charged material for a living — and she does the same thing to her own pouches after a wet week, because damp accelerant is worse than none.

Her working day is preparation. Bag casters are the ones who arrive early. A bag caster walking a site before a job is doing what her master did: finding out what the room is made of *now*, so that the thing she carries is the thing that will be needed.

And on a march she walks apart. The trade's own practice is that accelerant travels in the middle of a column and never on the solver — and she is the person for whom that rule cannot be obeyed, because the accelerant is what she is. She knows it. Everyone in the column knows it. Nobody says anything about it and everybody leaves a gap.

### What it costs

She is never short by accident. She is only ever short by somebody else's hand in her stores, and that is a real and common way to be ruined: a tenth part of something else mixed through, and the recognition is off by exactly the margin that matters.

And the deeper cost is what she spent her life learning. She knows her pouches by heart. She has never had to learn a wall. The day the supply stops she is a novice in her own room — still gifted, still fast on anything she is carrying, and reading fittings under pressure at whatever her acuity happens to be, which is the exact thing she bought powder to avoid.

Old bag casters are wealthy or they are nothing, and there is not much in between. A supply chain is a business, and a business either survives its owner's bad decade or it does not.

## 5. The Instrument

**Casts with an object he has read through.** A rod, a gauntlet, armour — and at the far end, metal set into him.

### How you come to it

Bought, or inherited, and the difference between those two is most of a man's biography.

Somebody bored the rod. Somebody sold it. Somebody read it for him before he paid, because an unread rod is a stick, and a rod that is not what the seller says it is will fail in the middle of a stroke. There is an entire small trade in reading instruments for buyers, and it is exactly as reputable as any other trade in vouching for what a thing is.

At the far end of the tradition somebody cut him open. Augmentation is a respectable branch of the work with surgeons, a price list, and a recovery time. It is also the point at which a family's opinion arrives: there is a first time a mother sees metal in her son's arm, and the trade is entirely used to that conversation and has stopped being surprised by how it goes.

### How you live

With a maker's name on the thing in his hand, and that name is nearly as load-bearing as his master's.

Rods are inherited. Rods are argued over in wills. A rod that has been in a family three generations is worth more than the same object made last year, and the reason given is that it is known — read, tested, and never once wrong. Whether that is true or sentiment is a thing practitioners argue about, in print and in taprooms, the way they argue about everything.

The implant is the other half of the tradition and it changes his standing entirely. Carried sap makes a man independent of pools and supply lines, which is precisely why companies like him: he can work where there is nothing, and he does not need their drawing floor, and he does not stop when the haulier is late. It is also why he wears sleeves. Frost and heat at the seam is the easiest thing in the world to read across a room, and a man who does not want to be read covers the join.

### What it costs

The money, first, and it is a real gate. This is the one tradition you can be locked out of by being poor, and everybody knows which families produce instrument casters.

Then the seat. Grey skin, two fingers' width around the join, once it has taken an overdraw — permanent, and visible to anyone he ever undresses in front of.

And then the standing fact of the tradition: everything he casts with can be swapped, fouled, or pulled off him. A swapped capsule is the cruellest thing that can be done to a caster, because he sets what he thinks he packed, and he finds out in the same moment as everyone standing near him. He can buy a line that never smudges. He can buy a charge already loaded. He cannot buy acuity, and no amount of money has ever changed that, and it is the one thing that keeps the tradition honest.

## 6. The Body

**Casts with herself.** The line is cut into her own skin, so she is always inside her own boundary — and anything inside the line is a source.

### How you come to it

Usually nobody taught her.

There was no rod to buy. There was no master who would take her — because a master's name is a thing he is careful with, and taking on a girl from a channel gang with no money and no connection is a risk he does not have to run. There was nobody to stand over her and make her say the line out loud.

So she used the one thing she had, which was herself, and she found out what it cost afterwards, in stages, alone.

This is not always the story. Some body casters are taught, and a few districts have a genuine tradition of it passed between women who have no other option and know exactly what they are handing on. But the typical case is a person who solved something she needed solved, at fifteen, with her hand flat on it and nothing else in reach, and who did not know until much later that there had been another way to do it.

### How you live

Cheap to employ and impossible to hold. No supplier can cut her off. No maker can price her out. No academy can decline to vouch. No gang cutting a channel forty miles upstream can touch her, because she is not drawing on their water. In a world where every other tradition rests on something somebody else owns, she rests on nothing.

And she pays for that in the most public currency there is. Every caster's boundary shows dust-fine when the stroke fires; hers is the only one you can read with no stroke thrown at all — scored, healed, and sitting on her arm in daylight. The trade can tell at a glance roughly how much she has already spent and roughly how much she has left. So can her landlord. So can the man deciding whether to hire her.

The world's reaction to this is not fear. It is discomfort. She is hurting herself, visibly and permanently, and most people find it hard to be near. They are polite to her. They pay her. They do not invite her back. That is the taboo, and it is worse than fear would be, because fear at least treats a person as significant.

### What it costs

She is inside every line she draws, so every working she throws short takes her own water and lime before it takes anything in the room. Cramps first. Then teeth. Then bone. Staged, cumulative, and it never comes back — and it is not overdraw, and it does not harden, which is why the trade reads a body caster at a glance by her teeth rather than by her hands.

And a scored line is permanent. A new line crossing an old one breaks the run before it closes, so she cannot write on the same skin twice. Her working map only ever shrinks: forearm, then the other forearm, then the thigh, then the back. Nobody lets it past the collarbone, because past the collarbone is the throat, and everyone who has ever been in the trade knows what that means and has heard of somebody who did it anyway.

An old body caster is a rare thing and everyone who meets one knows they are looking at somebody who made a series of decisions.

## 7. The Scholar

**Casts with what she remembers.** Every material she has already read and retained.

### How you come to it

Out of other people's records first, and then by hand — and the second half is the part that is not optional.

She reads what somebody else wrote down, and then she goes and touches the thing, because paper is only an index. A record can tell her *which* of the things she has already touched this is. It cannot put a material into library, ever, and every practitioner in the world knows it, which is why a scholar's education is largely travel and largely tedious.

Her path is the only one that can be started late and started alone, which is why it collects a particular kind of person: the second son, the widow, the clerk who was better at this than at what she was doing. It is also the slowest.

### How you live

Near an institution, or arguing with one.

The Table of families is published, disputed and incomplete. Nobody holds the whole list, several entries on it are certainly two families filed as one, and a working practitioner meets an unfiled family perhaps twice in a career. So there are rival lists. There are academies that back one and refuse the other. There are careers spent on a single disputed entry, and a family that has held one for three generations, and field practitioners who use a version the academies say is wrong and who are, sometimes, right.

Her library cannot be stolen, cut, adulterated or pulled off her strap. She travels well and she is welcome in most places, and she is the tradition most likely to end up in a room where decisions are made, because comprehension is the trade's progression and she is visibly further along it than anyone else present.

### What it costs

It takes a life, and the list is never finished. That is not a lament, it is the design: a complete Table would turn mastery into memorisation, and the reason comprehension is the axis is precisely that nobody can ever reach the end of it.

And her whole speed is recognising. Swap the fittings and she works at full speed, confidently, on a material that is not there — and then she finds out, and now she has to read the thing *and* disprove what she already knows, which is slower than her own novice self was. A veteran in a substituted room is worse off than a novice in it. Everybody in the trade knows this and it is done to scholars deliberately.

## 8. The Attuned

**Casts with whatever is under her palm.**

### How you come to it

Before she can remember.

Not a bloodline — nobody's blood owns the read, and any suggestion otherwise is a thing outsiders say about them rather than a thing that is true. What they are is a people who put children's hands on things from the moment those children can walk, and correct them, every day, for years, without ever calling it teaching.

It is not learned the way a trade is learned. There is no indenture, no term, and no master. It is learned the way a language is learned: by everyone around you, constantly, informally, and completely — and, like a language, it cannot be picked up later at the same depth by somebody who came to it at twenty.

### How you live

On ground you know. Gate 1 makes known ground real and free and deliberately non-portable, and the attuned are what that rule looks like when a whole community organises around it.

She is the one who notices. A palm on stone tells her whether it is at rest, forced out of rest, or already worked — and on ground she has knelt on for twenty years, she feels the surplus coming up slower than it used to. She says so a season before a drawing floor can prove anything and a year before the wells go hard.

And nobody has ever taken her word for it. That is the whole political fact of the tradition, stated once and not laboured: the only people in the world who can feel a basin being cut upstream are the people whose word carries no weight with anyone who could stop it.

Their ground was taken. It happened, legally, by people who filed the correct paperwork, and it is not a mystery to be uncovered — it is a thing the older ones remember and the younger ones were told about. What it did to particular families is available to the story, and belongs to whoever's back-story needs it. The land itself, and who now holds the paper on it, is Gate 5.

### What it costs

No library worth the name. She was taught to feel and never taught to file, so she reads everything from scratch, every time, and recognising is the whole of the fast tier. Large means chained and chained means from memory, so everything she takes has to fit inside one working.

And her whole advantage is the ground underneath her — which means it ends on the day she is moved off it. She is not diminished by exile in some poetic sense. She is measurably, mechanically worse at her work in a place she does not know, and this world's industry moves people off ground for a living.

## 9. Where the Traditions Meet

They meet as trades meet: over money, hiring, marriage, and who gets the blame.

**The bag and the instrument** are the two that prepare, and they compete for the same work and the same reputation. A works choosing between them is choosing between a woman who must be resupplied and a man who cost a great deal once. The argument is old, both sides have a case, and it is usually settled by which of them the foreman already knows.

**The scholar and everyone else** is a question of deference. She is slower to arrive and better to have, and field practitioners resent being corrected by someone who has not been on a floor in eleven years. When she is right, which is often, they resent it more.

**The attuned and the industry** is not a rivalry. It is a grievance, and only one side experiences it as a relationship at all.

**And the body against all of them** is the ugly one, because it is mostly about not being in the room. She is not turned away at the door. She is simply not thought of when the work is being handed out, and when she is hired it is for the jobs where somebody expects a bad outcome and would rather not spend a caster with a name on it.

Marriage is where the traditions are least polite about each other. A bag family marries an instrument family and both sides regard it as a merger. Nobody's family is pleased about a body caster, and the reasons given aloud are never the real one.

## 10. What It Has Culminated To

Put together, the traditions describe a trade that has organised itself entirely around two facts: the work is indispensable, and the people who can do it are very few and get hurt.

Everything in this document is what a world builds when it needs a scarce skill badly and has no way to regulate it. It cannot license, so it vouches. It cannot inspect, so it asks who taught you. It cannot manufacture casters, so it recruits from everywhere and takes what the lottery hands it. It cannot protect them, so it grades their materials, tests their stock in public, and keeps its accelerant in the middle of the column.

And the shape it has arrived at is not equal. Four of the five traditions rest on something somebody else owns — stock, makers, academies, ground — which means four of the five can be shut off by a person who never enters the room. Only the body rests on nothing, and the price of that is a life spent visibly, in stages, in public, at the bottom of everyone's regard.

That is where Gate 2 leaves the world: a trade with no law over it, held together by names, sitting on top of an ordinary industrial economy of people who cannot cast at all — and one tradition inside it whose independence is indistinguishable, from the outside, from self-destruction.
