Project Chimera · Gate 2 · the schools, doctrines and traditions of casting

Taught, or Found

Nobody in this world picked a school. They were taught by whoever was standing there — or there was nobody standing there, and they worked it out. Five traditions, doing different things with the same two strokes, because of who was in the room when they learned.

There is no licence. There is a name.

The credential

Who taught you?

Nobody licenses casting. No inspector, no seal, no register — a field surgeon setting a fracture in forty seconds cannot wait on paperwork, and the state has never found a way to stand between a person and her own two hands. What the world has instead is a question, and everyone in the trade asks it inside the first minute.

What it doesA name is a credential. Who taught you — and who taught them — is the whole of a caster's standing, and it travels further than any certificate would. A good name gets you hired sight unseen. A bad one follows you between towns.

Why it holdsBecause the trade has a very high rate of injury and everybody knows it. Vouching for someone is putting your own name behind the claim that she says her source out loud, every time. That is worth more than an inspection nobody could staff.

Who it locks outAnyone with no name to give. The body caster taught herself because nobody would take her — so when she is asked, she has no answer, and the answer she doesn't have is written up her arm where everyone can see it.

The five

Casts with · carried reagents

The bag

Flint, carbon, salts, filings, and accelerant — graded, weighed, and paid for. She weighs before she names, because the proportion is the only thing she owns that the room does not.

How you come to itApprenticed. Somebody stood over her and made her say the line out loud until it was a habit she could not drop — and made her say which lime, and how much, until "enough" stopped being an answer.

How you liveInside a supply chain. Somebody mills it, somebody grades it, somebody cuts it. She has a supplier she trusts and one she doesn't, and she draws a bead in the shop like everyone else. On a march she walks where nobody wants to walk near her.

What it costs youShe has spent a life learning her pouches instead of learning rooms. The day the supply stops she is a novice in her own room — and the accelerant that made her fast is the nearest source in that room, on her own hip.

Casts with · an object he has read through

The instrument

A rod, a gauntlet, armour — and at the far end, metal set into him. Never a device. Only ever a material he already knows by touch, doing the touching for him.

How you come to itBought, or inherited. Somebody bored the rod, somebody sold it, and somebody read it for him before he paid. At the far end somebody cut him open to seat it — augmentation is a respectable branch of the trade, with surgeons and a price list.

How you liveWith a maker's name on the thing in his hand. A rod is inherited, argued over in wills, and read before it is trusted. An implant buys independence from pools and supply, which is exactly why the companies like him — and why he wears sleeves.

What it costs youThe money first, and it is a real gate. Then the seat: grey skin two fingers around the join, once it has taken an overdraw. And everything he casts with can be swapped, fouled, or pulled off him.

Casts with · her own skin

The body

The line is cut into her, so she is always inside her own boundary — and anything inside the line is a source. Hers is the only account in the trade written where strangers can read it.

How you come to itUsually nobody taught her. There was no rod to buy, no master who would take her, and nobody to say the line over her — so she used the one thing she had, and found out what it cost afterwards, in stages.

How you liveCheap to employ and impossible to hold. She needs no supplier, no maker, no academy, and no ground she knows. Nobody upstream can shut her off. Everyone can see exactly how much she has already spent.

What it costs youEverything she throws short takes her own water and lime — cramps, then teeth, then bone, and it never comes back. A scored line is permanent, so her map only shrinks: forearm, other forearm, thigh, back. Nobody lets it past the collarbone.

Casts with · what she remembers

The scholar

Every material she has already read and retained. Paper is only an index — it tells her which of the things she has touched this is. Nothing enters library except through her own fingers.

How you come to itOut of other people's records first, then by hand. She reads what someone else wrote down, then goes and touches the thing, because a record is not a read and every practitioner knows it.

How you liveNear an institution, or arguing with one. The Table is published, disputed and incomplete — so there are rival lists, careers spent on one family, and real fights about what belongs on it. Her library cannot be stolen, so she travels well and is welcome most places.

What it costs youIt takes a life, and the list is never finished. And her whole speed is recognising — so swap the fittings and she works at full speed, confidently, on a material that isn't there. Then she finds out.

Casts with · whatever is under her palm

The attuned

Not a bloodline — nobody's blood owns the read. A people who put children's hands on things from the moment they can walk, and correct them, every day, for years.

How you come to itBefore she can remember. It is not taught as a trade with an indenture and a term. It is taught the way a language is taught — by everyone around her, constantly, badly, and completely.

How you liveOn ground she knows. She is the one who feels the surplus coming up slower than it did — a season before a drawing floor can prove anything, and a year before the wells go hard. Nobody has ever taken her word for it.

What it costs youNo library worth the name; she was taught to feel and never taught to file. And her whole advantage is the ground underneath her, which means it ends the day she is moved off it — and moving people off ground is what this world's industry does.

Settled

All five are answered and recorded in the decisions log. Gate 2 is closed. What each one buys is below.

LicensingNo licence — but a lineage

What was decidedCasting is not licensed by anyone. The credential is who taught you: schooled, then apprenticed to a master, and the master's name is the thing that travels

What it buysStanding is social, not legal, so it can be given and withdrawn by people rather than by a state. And it hands the body school its exclusion for free — she has no name to give

StatusThe body is the taboo one

What was decidedShe is hurting herself, visibly and permanently, and the world finds that hard to be near. Not feared — uncomfortable. People are polite to her and do not invite her back

What it buysThe most self-reliant caster alive is the lowest-status one, and the reason is written where nobody can pretend not to see it. That contradiction is a character, not a rule

The attunedTheir ground was already taken

What was decidedIt happened, and it is available as a turn rather than a premise — something a character carries and does not lead with. Held for the reveal, and for whoever's back-story needs it

What it buysA grievance that needs no villain, and a piece of history the audience meets through a person instead of a map. The land itself is Gate 5; what it did to a family is Gate 2's to keep

How manyFive, and the door stays open

What was decidedFive is right. More can be added later. The layers-out and the extractor men are rejected — hauling, channelling and burying are manual labour, not casting, and the people doing them mostly have no gift at all

What it buysThe correction is worth more than the schools were. It establishes that the sap economy runs on non-casters, which is the class structure of the whole setting

The giftA slope — and it is that steep

What was decidedGate 1 stands unamended. The slope is kept, and it is so steep at the low end that most people will never cast a single thing in their lives. "The gift" is the folk word for the top of it — the village says she has the gift, the academy says unusual acuity, started young. Same girl, two vocabularies

What it buysThe felt reality of rarity, arrived at by cost and ceiling rather than by permission — which is what Gate 1 required. And it makes the gift a lottery, not a lineage: it turns up in any family, no house can hold it, and the trade recruits from everywhere because it has to
