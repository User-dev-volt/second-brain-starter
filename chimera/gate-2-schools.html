The Five Schools

Pinch hand · Hafter · Carrier · Bookhand · Ground-walker

A school is not a spell list and not a faction. It is the answer to one question: what do you cast with. Every caster in the world obeys the same two strokes and the same four failure modes, but the substance a working consumes has to come from somewhere, and there are only so many places to get it — what you brought, what you're holding, what you are, what you remember, or what's under your feet. The trade splits on that because the answer decides everything downstream: what your hands do, what you carry, what you're worth to hire, what beats you, and what's left of you at fifty.

How a person reads is personal style inside a school, never the thing that defines it. Cutting a chip, sounding through a bar, walking ground the night before, reading a body's vent, reading the record — two casters of the same school can work completely differently and both be orthodox. It is colour. It is not the axis.

Three rulings the set now obeys, because four schools were quietly breaking them:

  • A chain contains a solve. There is no such thing as a "chained set." Every school's FAST tier costs a full solve and a quarter, which makes the fast tier the expensive tier for everybody. What separates the schools is how many they can afford and what cheap work they do in between. Most of them live on the plain set — a tenth the cost, no chain, no solve, on matter already loose or already re-tying.
  • Paper is an index, not a library. Library is still what a hand has touched and retained. What a record does is tell you which of the things you have already touched the thing in front of you is. A bookhand who has handled four hundred of that mill's window cramps recognises the four hundred and first; she has never recognised anything she has no sibling for.
  • No instrument reads. An implant is a sap topology. A rod is a boundary and a magazine. Nothing worn, seated or carried perceives anything. Acuity is not for sale.

·Pinch hand

Casts with: stock she milled, graded, dried and packed herself, carried in counted pockets on her body.

The doctrine. She never asks a wall what it is, because a wall can lie and her own sieve cannot. When the board runs out she works the room like anybody else — badly, at a novice's speed, with no fast tier at all — and she hates every second of it. She thinks the others are squatters: brilliant at home, tourists everywhere, and one day they will fight in a room that has never met them.

Topology · better stroke. Flask · set, overwhelmingly. Her staple costs a tenth of a solve and needs no chain, because a pinch of milled powder is already parted — she is not undoing a hold, she is tying one. And because a boundary is any closed run of contact, a pinch thrown so it lands as a closed splash is a boundary she did not have to walk to. Distance is a boundary problem.

THE IMAGE. A chest-and-belly board of two dozen stitched pockets under a leather harness, each pocket edge filed with a count of nicks so the thumb finds pocket six in the dark at a run; she fights bent forward, chin down over her own chest, and she leaves lines behind her — grey, rust-red, bone-white powder laid across floors and doorframes and up the front of a man's coat. By the end of a fight you can read her trouble from across the room, because you can count how many pockets are hanging flat and open.

The three speeds. - Staple (0.5 s, moving, cheap, most of what she does). Thumb rides the nicks, hooks a flap, two fingers in, four grams out, thrown underarm to land closed across a boot and the flag under it. Plain set. Heat, one snap, the resin goes up and the boot with it. Her left hand never stops guarding. She cannot do this and hold a weapon; the pour is the tempo, one per exchange, and she is defenceless through it. - FAST (0.9 s, moving, chained, a whole swallow). When the thing that needs parting is already in front of her and she cannot bring one — a bar across a door, a hasp, a blade. Solve and set in one gesture from memory, at full price and a quarter. Three or four of these is her day. - HEAVY (9 s, one knee down, left palm flat on the flagstone the whole time). Right hand empties a full belt pocket as a closed ring a yard across, near arc laid last so the circle only closes when she is ready. Nobody standing inside. She checked. A slab stands out of the floor at chest height. - PREPARED (two hours). Walks the ground with a horn of cheap lime, laying boundaries she does not fire — rings under the rushes at each doorway, a long open run down the gutter with the low end pointing at the stable wall. Then repacks the board for the room she has just seen: heavy families to the belt, fire to the top row, everything else left in the pack, because the difference between a nine-kilo board and an eleven-kilo board is whether she can still run at the end.

What it is bad at. Mass — she has what she brought, and everything she does subtracts, so her big workings come out small, deliberately voided, or reaching. Tempo — she cannot pour with her hands full, and a fighter who keeps her hands full ends her. Ground — a thrown boundary only closes on flat footing at short range; on rushes, mud, water, grass, wind or a moving man it lands open, and an open run set short is a runner she did not intend. Damp — a wet crossing drops half the board a grade and she finds out when a set stutters. Salting — she never reads her own stock, so swap the contents of pocket six and she works at full speed, confidently, on a family that is not there.

Beaten by. The hafter, because reloading is time with her hands full of powder and he has no reload; he does not out-think her, he out-tempos her until she pours short. The carrier, in a room neither prepared, because nine kilos of loose graded material rides on the outside of her body and everyone knows where it is — one palm-ring closed on the harness strap ends her. The ground-walker, on ground the walker has walked, because the walker chooses what a short working finds and the nearest maximally-out-of-rest matter in the room is the board on the pinch hand's chest.

Socially. Hired by people going somewhere they have never been: road escorts, salvage crews on a building nobody has a plan of, boarding parties. Not hired by anyone counting weight and food. Paid twice — the client buys the stock in advance by weight and grade, she will show you the sieve, and then pays a fee on top; what leaves the board is charged whether it worked or not, because it is gone either way. The other trades call them spenders. They call everyone else squatters. Both sides think this is funny and neither has ever laughed.

Magnifying. Owns accelerant outright — she is accelerant made portable and honest, and the whole grading vocabulary is her working language. Owns fire, cheapest by weight, which is why four grams is a serious threat. Owns the runner and dies of it: she can pour a run across mud or a deck where nobody else can lay one, and she is standing at the near end of it wearing the loosest, most willing material within thirty feet. Cannot do deep extraction at all — that is a sustained draw off a seat and she is a magazine with a count. Every pinch hand wears a feed pocket low and outboard, the cheapest coarsest stock she owns, put there to be eaten first.

What a lifetime does. Overdraw eats what was under load, and hers is a short list: the pinching thumb and two fingers, the wrist, the strip of collarbone under the harness. Slack shows as waste, not weakness — the tremor makes her pours run long and she notices it in the stock count before she notices it in her hand. Thinning takes the thumb-web on the right and leaves the left hand normal, which is what frightens the young ones. Hardening blooms grey-white in three nail beds and in a band along the collarbone, and the collarbone sounds wrong when tapped; they all tap it, a knuckle against the chest while thinking, and everyone in the trade knows what it means. Failure is the fingers stopping. An old pinch hand pours from a spoon, works left-handed, weighs everything aloud, and arrives early because she no longer runs.

The internal split. Pourers fight out of the pockets, spend the board and resupply after — fast, expensive, hired for one job. Layers put nearly everything on the floor before contact and fight standing in a room they have already declared, and consider a pourer a woman doing at speed what she failed to do in advance. (Milling versus buying is a separate quarrel and mostly about money.)

Scene. The doorway is holding and pocket nine is empty. Left palm flat on the sill, staying there. Right thumb walks the board edge — four nicks, five, six. Spar and lime, three to one, milled Tuesday. She pours it as a run, not a ring, twelve paces up the passage, wrist low. Eight hundred grams, near enough, and near enough is short. The line goes dust-fine along its whole length. Heat, one snap, and the drag opens behind it, curving off the wall toward the packed bodies at the far end because they are the densest thing down there. Both hands empty now. She keeps them empty and counts what is left.

·Hafter

Casts with: the object in his hand — a bored rod, a palm-plate, a barred crowbar, and at the far end the metal seated into his arm at nineteen.

The doctrine. He does not put his hands on the world, he puts the thing on the world, and the thing has a boundary cut permanently into its face that no rain and no boot will ever smear. When the instrument fails him he changes the tip, and if he cannot change the tip he leaves, because a hafter without an instrument is a man with a heavy sleeve and an opinion. Of the others he says: nothing on the skin — they are one wet floor and one bad guess from finding out what their own hands are made of.

Topology · better stroke. Implant · set. No count, no line, no ground going grey behind him; he chains at a rate no flask and no pool can hold, for exactly as long as the seat holds. His staple is a plain set through the scored ring onto stock packed in a bore — cheap, instant, and the most repeatable declaration in the trade, because the ring is true and dead centre every single time.

THE IMAGE. The man in a warm room with his sleeves buttoned at the wrist, who opens doors with the end of a rod and never touches anything, standing square with one hand mid-shaft and the other flat on the butt-cap, eyes somewhere else while he listens. In cold air there is a thread of steam off his cuff that nothing else in the room is doing.

The three speeds. - Staple (0.4 s, moving, cheap). Metal thumb walks the index rings on the shaft to the wire bore without looking, wrist rolls the tip onto the door-strap, set. A bar across the frame that was not there. He does not stop walking. - FAST (0.8–1.2 s, moving, chained, full price and a quarter). The same gesture where something has to be parted first — a hinge, a hasp, a blade held against him — from library, on straps he has worked in this ironmonger's for six years. - HEAVY (8 s, planted). The only time a hafter touches the world: his off hand goes flat on the brick and stays there the whole eight seconds while he stacks his weight through the instrument's face beside it. Every hafter hates this, and every hafter learns something in those eight seconds he would rather not have known. The wall's window is a moment and it re-holds in a day and a half, so he gets to be wrong once. - PREPARED (bench work, which is most of the job). He bores, packs, weighs and seals; re-cuts the ring with a graver until it bites; wipes the face with spirits; checks the seat with a wet cloth and dries it. Then he kinks wire loops to lengths he has read and memorised so each lands open into a closed run, lays one under the rushes an hour early, and sets it from six metres away with his hands nowhere near it.

What it is bad at. He works at the end of a tool, which means that at speed he is not touching the thing he is changing — so he works from what he assumes it is, and everything the school does rests on being right about that in advance. He can put a bare palm on it and get families, proportion and state like anybody; doing so costs him the plant, the tempo and the reason he is a hafter, and in a fight he does not. Half-set is therefore his specific death: it looks like matter, it takes the hold out of any contact surface, and it eats the tip, then the shaft, then it is travelling toward the port at his wrist. Every hafter has practised the drill; about a third have needed it. The seat steams, is an advertised target, scatters under a flat blow, and a hand on him with a wet strap over the seam makes him a civilian in one second. And the bores are the most out-of-rest matter in the room, bolted to his arm.

Beaten by. The carrier, at contact range, because she is the only school whose fast tier works the thing you are holding: steel is deep in her library, she closes the palm-ring on the rod, and he is left with a heavy sleeve. The bookhand, because she chooses the building — she knows which pillar was patched, which flight was rebuilt with rolled steel after the fire, which fittings were re-hung when the supplier changed, and every one of those is a thing he will close on at full chained speed on the assumption that it is what it looks like.

Socially. Yards, drill crews, mine heads, bridge and dock work, and houses that want a quiet man in the hall. Nobody who works wet will take one — no ship's crew signs a hafter, and he is the first to say so — and no salvage crew picking over old workings will have him at any price. Paid by the job, half up front, and famously paid twice: once for the work, and once, for eleven years, for the arm. The seating is done by a seater, a bone-man and a smith over one table, and the good ones are known by name in three trades. The class quarrel is that an implant does not care what you were born with, so a second son with money and a steady nerve gets the tempo of a duellist and a woman with better hands never will. The bare-hand trades call his topology rented. He calls theirs weather-dependent.

Magnifying. Owns accelerant storage — nobody keeps powder like a man whose powder lives inside sealed steel, and nobody carries the room's nearest source closer to his own bone. Owns small fire absolutely: a precise, aimed, contemptibly small flame he can put in a thrown wire ring six metres out or at the bottom of a shaft. Cannot make sheet fire; his boundary is a coin. Cannot lay a runner — his boundary is closed by definition and cut into metal, and laying an open run by hand is the one thing this school never practises; the yards treat a hafter laying a runner as a man who has decided something about himself. Owns deep extraction outright, coupled to a drill string, which is simply the longest instrument ever made — and because nobody has read what is down there, it is a reach, aimed downward, never a chain.

What a lifetime does. Overdraw eats what was under load, and what is under load is never his hands — it is the seam. Slack is an ache at the seat that stays, a shortened stride on that side, and a fine tremor in the other hand from twenty years of holding the cap steady. Thinning is grotesquely asymmetric: the upper arm above the port wastes to the bone while the sound arm is a bricklayer's. Hardening blooms grey-white at the rim of the port and in that elbow crease, cold and gritty — and he has been tapping his own forearm for thirty years pretending he was testing the rod, so the morning the note goes wrong he knows exactly what he is listening to. Failure is the seat refusing to take, dead meat around live metal, and it has to come off higher, and there is often not enough left to seat into again. An old hafter wears his sleeves buttoned in July and lets an apprentice carry the case. His instruments are older than his seat, tips dished from a hundred re-cuts, wax gone amber in the bores; they are worth more than new ones and they work worse, so he runs on library, and he can name every material that rod has ever held.

The internal split. Packers fill every bore before they go out, on the principle that you name your source by carrying it, and accept that they are carrying it on their own arm. Dry men run the bores empty on purpose, name nothing, and let each working find its own source — which packers consider murder practising to become a habit.

Scene. He has read this cellar a hundred times. Metal hand mid-shaft, thumb walking the index rings to the lime bore, off hand flat on the pillar, weight forward. Eight seconds is the price and he is four in when the tip goes warm. Brick is not warm. The shaft has gone matte to the grip, no shine anywhere on it, the edges shimmering — he is not holding a rod, he is holding the thing that eats rods, and it is nine inches from the port. He lets go. It slumps against his boot and takes the sole with it. Left thumb under the belt. Wet strap off, over the seam, pull. Civilian, till morning.

·The carry

Casts with: herself — the sap comes off a pool or a flask, crosses her flesh, and leaves through a closed run cut permanently into her skin.

The doctrine. She never draws a line because she is one, so she can work the instant she can touch the thing, with nothing in her hands and nobody with her. When a route greys out and will not carry any more she cuts new marks somewhere else on the body and starts again, and she does that until there is nowhere left worth spending, and then she sets down. Of everyone else she says: fine casters, all of them, so long as nobody takes the table away.

Topology · better stroke. Pool, drawn without plumbing · set. She cannot find a pool — that is the walker's trade — but she can drink from any pool she can put a hand in: no gate, no basin, no channel work, no permission, which is why pool-owners will not have a grey-arm within sight of a sill. Away from water she carries a flask, and a flask is three or four workings and every carrier hates it. Set is carried through the boundary rather than the skin, and for her the boundary is skin, so her set runs the shortest route any caster has and costs a tenth. Solve costs everything and the crossing is permanent, so the mature practice is to part with tools — a bar, a chisel, fire, a partner — and set herself. A carrier with a crowbar in her off hand is not a carrier who has run out. She is a carrier who intends to live.

THE IMAGE. Sleeves gone at the shoulder in any weather, both bare forearms carrying closed runs cut into the skin — rings round the palms, a long loop up the inside of the arm — and above the marks a chalk-coloured bloom creeping out of the wrist toward the elbow, sharp-edged as a tideline. She does not crouch to draw and does not step back to a table: she walks into contact, palm flat on the thing, and for a quarter-second she is outlined in pale dust from fingertip to collarbone.

The three speeds. - Staple (0.4 s, contact, cheap). Somebody else parted it — a hammer, a bar, a fire, a collapse already going. She sets. She does this all day and it costs her almost nothing but the passage. - FAST (0.9 s, moving, chained, full price and a quarter, and it is what ages her). A man comes at her with a sword and she catches the blade in her bare palm, closing the ring on it: steel, deep in library, under a quarter-second, state free. One gesture, set short on purpose, and she hands the blade back full-sized and forty percent hollow. It rings flat and folds across his own guard on the next swing. Her hand is open to the bone and she has spent one passage. - HEAVY (9 s, stationary). Both forearms flat on a wall she walked past that morning, shoulder to wrist, and they stay flat — the moment either arm lifts, the solve has not happened and the sap is gone. A bag of quicklime and a bucket of water sit against her boots, inside the run, banked so that a shortfall takes them first. The cold comes up both arms to the elbows and sits there for an hour, thick and stupid. - PREPARED (an hour, out of combat). Cord work: a loop of wire run under a collapsed floor, both ends clamped into the palm rings so her marks and the wire become one closed run — and now the boundary declares that whole circuit, and everything standing inside it, as what the working takes first. Prepared work does not make a carrier stronger. It decides what she takes from. (Cutting a new site is not a prepared working; it is a body project measured in weeks.)

What it is bad at. Range — everything is contact, and at four metres she is an unarmed woman with cut-up arms. Duration — every other caster's cost resets and hers does not, so carriers disengage while there is arm left, and everyone who has not thought about it reads that as cowardice. Substitution — she is the most library-dependent caster alive, because her speed is chaining and chaining fires against recognition and cannot be aborted; plate the door, face the wall, swap the fitting, and she commits at full speed into material that is not there. Half-set — her contact surface is her hand, so a single sack of half-set flung across a doorway shuts her out of a room completely, and she knows it, and she will not step through. No crew — nobody can steer a carrier's set, because there is nowhere to put a second pair of hands.

Beaten by. The pinch hand, when the pinch hand was in the building first: her own plate on a doorframe, her own billets on a landing, her own bolts through a rail, and the carrier recognises what she studied and fires into somebody else's substance. The ground-walker, because a walker's routine output is half-set on the floor and she can lay it across every route into the room; the carry does not fight through half-set, it simply does not go.

Socially. Mine rescue first and always — under a fall, with the roof still working, a scribed hand goes down with no kit and no crew and starts the moment she can touch stone, and everybody in a mining district knows the name of theirs. Then field surgery, wrecking, dock work, and the extraction outfits, who hire them by the season and use them up. Not hired by anyone who needs the job to look respectable, houses with children in them, or pool-owners. Paid by the passage: before she starts she names how many times sap will have to cross her — four, nine, twenty-two — and the whole of the haggle is arguing that number down, which does not change what the job takes, only what she gets for it. Rescue work is mostly unpaid, and a carrier who has pulled people out of a fall in a district never afterward pays for food or a bed in it. What the trades say is "she'll do it now," with real respect, and "grey to the collar by thirty, and still the first one down the shaft," which is admiration and a warning and an epitaph in one breath.

Magnifying. Owns accelerant, and not for the usual reason: it lowers the stroke cost, which matters more to her than to anyone because her cost is permanent — but the real use is that accelerant is maximally out of rest and so is a living channel with sap crossing it. Wear your lime low is the first thing a carrier is taught, and it does not mean for convenience. Cannot do fire on the body: the set delivers heat at her own skin, so a carrier who sets accelerant in her palm has set it in her palm, and you can identify the ones who learned this the practical way by the burn scarring over marks that no longer close. Cannot lay a runner — start an open run at her marks and the densest thing at the head of the line is her; the carry cedes the runner entirely. Owns deep extraction and it is the ugly heart of the school: flat on the working floor, both forearms and her forehead on stone, reaching, no boundary work, no crew, the ground under her and she is the channel. Extraction hands do a handful of seasons and come out grey from fingertip to jaw. The outfits do not have trouble finding more.

What a lifetime does — two clocks, one substance. The grey is hardening, the same hardening every trade gets. For a carrier it arrives by two different roads and the readouts are different, and knowing which is which is the whole of the school's arithmetic.

The creep is a distance, and it is deterministic. Every passage advances the mark up the limb by a measurable amount, weighted by stroke — a set is small, a solve is large, a chain is large and a quarter — and it moves while you watch, at the moment of the working, not next spring. The gauge is how far it is to the next joint, and after that how far to the collarbone. Hardened tissue will not carry, so her working skin is a shrinking map: at the elbow she starts thinking about the next site, at the shoulder she has already cut it. Moving the channel costs three things — days of cutting and weeks before a new run closes reliably under load; months of being clumsy, which is the one thing she was never allowed to be; and geometry, because the forearm is the best site there is and everything after it is worse. A thigh means putting a knee on the work. Ribs mean turning your body into the thing you press against a wall. The back covers the most skin and greys slowest, and you cannot see your own back, so somebody else cuts it and it never closes quite where you asked. Four sites in a working life is a great many. Nobody in the trade lets it past the collarbone, because past the collarbone is the throat.

Overdraw is the other road and it is the canon one: slack, thinning, hardening, failure, jumped a whole stage by asking one working for more than the route could take. The creep is what the last nine jobs cost. The tremor is because she tried to lift a wall. An old one works sitting down with a heel against the skirting and both hands loose in her lap, and talks the count out loud to somebody younger.

The internal split. Wide-cut carriers hold that a large scribed run spreads each passage across more tissue and greys slower for the same work, so they cut early, deep and everywhere, and at twenty-five they look like something that should be in a bed. Single-ring carriers keep one small closed run in one palm and refuse to cut another as long as they live, holding that the creep follows the sap and not the scribing, and that every wide-cut who ever lived simply cast more because cutting made it easy to.

Scene. The floor above had come down in one piece across a man's legs. She went in flat, left palm on the slab — limestone, in library, a quarter-second — right palm on the sack of quicklime at her hip so the run closed through both and the sack stood inside the line. Solve. The shine died, the haze lifted and stood, the cold came up past her elbow. The sack went in one breath. Still short. She got her right hand onto the banked half-set at her knee before the dust-line jumped, and it jumped into that. The slab let go. The mark on her forearm sat a thumb's width higher than it had that morning, and she looked at it, and then she went back down.

·Bookhand

Casts with: the made world, held in memory by class — thousands of that mill's fittings, that yard's brick, that pattern's cramps — indexed by pattern books, mill marks and date stones so she knows which of the things she has already handled this one is.

The doctrine. Recognition arrives under a quarter-second at any acuity, so she works at full speed on objects she has never personally touched, provided she has touched enough of their siblings. When memory fails her she does not stand there reading — she takes her hand off, walks four paces to something she does know, and works that instead. Of the others she says: everyone works from something they did not make; the rest of them carry theirs in a satchel or under their skin where it can be taken off them.

Topology · better stroke. Flask · set. She owns the counting: the fight is arithmetic said aloud, and she is the only school whose audible tell is the sap number rather than the material. She knows the price of a working before she starts it, to the swallow, because she learned the cost alongside the make. Set consumes exact proportion — seven to three, and be right — and hers land at full substance with no void and no reach on material she has never handled. Solve she does well and can barely afford; a bookhand who solves twice in a room has spent the room, which is why she fights in buildings that are settling, burning, flooding or coming down, where things are already re-tying and a plain set will ride them.

THE IMAGE. A woman walking through a building at an ordinary pace, hands empty and swinging, touching nothing — no palm on the wall, no fingers trailing the rail, none of the surface-stroking every other school does constantly — talking steadily to herself in proportions with one thumb hooked on the flask stopper. The only time she touches anything is inside a single second: the hand shuts to a closed rim, comes flat for a fifth of a second as she passes, and the thing snaps behind her while she is already four paces on.

The three speeds. - Staple (0.5 s, walking, cheap). Plain sets on the class-standard: a hinge, a cramp, a rail, a stringer, a standard brick. She accepts the standard's own tolerance as deliberate void and does not care, because she knew the tolerance before she arrived. - FAST (1.0 s, on the move, chained, and it is a whole swallow). Approach, rim, a fifth of a second of contact, snap, four paces on. The gesture is one second; the contact is the smallest part of it. Three or four of these is a building. - HEAVY (7 s, kneeling, one hand flat on the stone and not lifting). The other hand on the flask, reciting the make of the thing out loud the whole time — lime, sand at five, cramps wrought and running back nine inches — because the recitation is her metronome, and a bookhand who stops talking mid-heavy has lost her place. - PREPARED (an afternoon, then a night). She goes to the demolition yard and sits with the salvage: handles a broken sash-weight, says its year, has the foreman confirm or correct her, copies a mill mark onto her cuff, argues for forty minutes about which season a works changed its zinc supplier, because seven-to-three and six-and-a-half-to-three-and-a-half are different workings and the second one reaches. Then she goes to the site at night and scores boundaries in advance — a groove round the base of every stanchion, a wire stapled in a closed run under the floor — so that tomorrow, under fire, there is nothing to draw.

What it is bad at. Anything not made to a standard: natural stone, weathered timber, fill, spoil, previously-worked matter, half-set, relics. She is fast on everything manufactured and slow on everything the world made or the trade already touched, and a district that has been fought over is illegible to her in exactly the places the fighting was worst. She cannot check — her own read is thin, twenty to forty seconds, families and proportion and no depth and no state, which in a fight does not exist, so a defender who calls across a yard we re-hung all of that in the spring has taken her out of the fast tier without touching anything and she has no way to find out if he is lying. Her errors always complete, because chained cannot be aborted. And hers is the only library that can be bought, forged or burned: a false date stone, a bad plate, a foreman paid to remember the wrong year, poisoning her months in advance in a room she will never enter.

Beaten by. The pinch hand, who is a walking substitution — she does not guess which fittings the bookhand memorised, she puts her own material into the building, and now the record is not merely useless but lethal. The carrier, who removes the building from the fight: the bookhand's speed is route, working things she passes, and inside a boundary the size of two people there is no next stanchion, and she cannot work a person.

Socially. Demolition and salvage hire her first and pay best, because bringing a wall down without taking the one behind it is exactly a question of knowing what the one behind it is. Then mills, mine works, bridge crews, sappers — anyone who can name a place and want someone who arrives already knowing it. Nobody hires her for new country, wrecks, or ground that has been fought over; the standing joke is that she is only ever right about places that already exist. She is paid twice and the second payment is the real one: sample pieces, cut sections, the run of a yard for a season, an hour alone with a date stone before the wall comes down. A bookhand at the end of a job negotiating for a crate of broken sash-weights is not being eccentric. The strong readers' line is they don't read, they remember, said with contempt, usually by a man who has just watched her run a whole building faster than he resolved a doorframe.

Magnifying. Owns the judgement of accelerant — fineness, dryness, purity, age is the same axis her whole memory runs on, and she can name a lime-burner by his mark and tell you his product is cut without opening the sack. Carries it warily and short, on a long strap set down at a distance before working, because she is the school whose short workings complete no matter what. Owns fire at the prepared tier and nowhere else: she scores closed runs into floors the night before, in rooms she will not be standing in, and fires them from a doorway by recognition. Owns the runner and will not lay one — she is the only caster who knows where the densest thing on each floor is without going to look, which is also why she will not lay one in a building she studied more than a season ago. Cannot do deep extraction: it is the one place in the world with no record, no mill, no mark and no year, and she casts with the record.

What a lifetime does. Overdraw eats what was under load and what a bookhand runs hardest is her mouth — she counts the price aloud through every working and drills proportions aloud for hours. Slack is the voice that runs out: she can still say the count, she cannot say it for the full seven seconds, and she starts abbreviating, which is how the crew knows before she does. Thinning wastes the jaw, tongue and throat asymmetrically, usually the side she turns to her crew, and the neck cords on whichever shoulder took the strap. Hardening blooms grey-white at both mouth-corners and along the jawline, and the jaw sounds wrong when tapped. Failure is that she cannot speak. An old bookhand is unmistakable: a wrecked lower face, a slate on a cord round her neck — and the best-preserved hands in the trade, uncallused and unmarked, because she never touched anything she did not have to. She writes the number on the slate and shows it to the crew before her hand goes flat. She carries her pattern books everywhere and never opens them.

The internal split. Orthodox bookhands work the published Table and publish their corrections into it, holding that a trade that hides its errors kills people it will never meet. Amenders keep private corrections and will not share them, holding that a proportion you paid for in somebody's death is not a gift, and that half the plates in circulation were wrong because somebody published in a hurry. The two want opposite things from the same object, and both can cite where they got it.

Scene. Third flight, and she says it before she touches it: "Grey iron, four at the string, cramps are wrought — take eleven." Thumb pops the stopper. The hand shuts to a rim and comes flat on the stringer. The snap is wrong — a stutter of them, four, five, going away down the stair. Somebody rebuilt this flight after a fire twelve years back, and they had rolled steel on the cart. She cannot abort. She feels the dust-line lift along her knuckles and jump, one frame, past her boot to the landing, where the boy with her chalk bag is standing with his hand out. She has time to say lime. She does not have time to move him.

·Ground-walker

Casts with: what is under the floor — found pools nobody surveyed, and the footing, fill and ground she has walked on her own knees.

The doctrine. She works from underneath, on ground she has covered placement by placement until it is in library, and she draws off pools she located herself with no basin and no gate. When the ground lies to her she gets off the working, moves twenty feet, and asks again from another placement, because a fill and a floor feel the same from one spot and different from three. Of the others she thinks they have learned the names of things instead of the things, and she is polite about it right up until somebody's well-briefed crew sinks a shaft into ground she told them was finished.

Topology · better stroke. Pool, found · solve — the only school in the set whose staple is the expensive stroke, which is exactly why she needs artillery topology under her. Solve is priced in sap and she is sitting on the supply; set is priced in knowing what a thing is made of, and she cannot name copper. So she has one set and it is put it back as itself, or put it back short on purpose. She takes things apart for a living and makes nothing.

THE IMAGE. A woman kneeling twenty feet from anyone with a flat pad under one knee, a staked cord run out across the flags and a nail in her fist for scratching grooves, working the ground while everybody else in the fight works at something waist-high — and the effect happening somewhere else, under the men, not under her. The grey and the frost-bite open out from her knees across the floor like weather, four feet, six feet, ten, until the far side of the room is white and the far wall is sweating.

The three speeds. - Staple — she has none worth the name. Her cheap stroke is the one she can't use: her set is a bare repair. A walker at rest is a walker spending nothing, and a walker working is a walker spending. - FAST (0.8–1.2 s, on the move, chained, full price and a quarter). Crossing her own yard she drops to one knee mid-stride, palm flat on a flag, and chains — solve and set in one gesture, no matte pause. The flag comes back voided to a shape she has done ten thousand times: a lip, a step, a ridge across a doorway. She can only do this on the six or ten things she has touched all her life, which is why known ground is the whole of the school. - HEAVY (9 s, stationary). Both palms to a wall footing, the boundary made by the rim of her own hands so the working takes the footing first and not her knees. The shine dies, the haze lifts and stands, the frost ring opens to arm's length, and the whole footing lets go at once. She does not set it back. She has no set worth the name for a wall. - PREPARED (ten minutes to hours). She arrives early and covers the ground on hands and knees, placement by placement, because her hands are short-range and her feet are the range. She finds where the pool comes nearest the surface and scratches a nail-groove round that patch; cuts a shallow catch so a thread ponds under where she means to kneel; solves a barrow of rubble into a heap of half-set and banks it beside the position. Then she tells the crew where to stand, in the only terms she has: here, not there, and don't go past the second window.

What it is bad at. She cannot make a thing — no brass, no wire, no glass, no patched armour, no fabrication of any kind, and anyone who picks this school expecting a builder has picked wrong. She has no magazine: shut a gate upstream, or fight her on a deck, an upper floor, or a paved yard over a dead channel, and she is a strong woman with cold hands. Off her own ground she has no chained working, which means no large working at all. She cannot brief a crew — she is a solver who cannot tell setters what she is doing in words they can act on, so she works alone or with three people who have known her twenty years. And her sense is short and slow: it is a palm on continuous ground and nothing else. A flask reads as a small hard fullness only when she has a hand on the man; through cloth at four paces she has nothing. Her certainty about a made floor feels identical to her certainty about a true one, and there is no check for that anywhere in the world.

Beaten by. The pinch hand, on ground neither has met, because the pinch hand carries her own recognition and the walker's is all in the floor of somewhere else — the walker has no fast tier at all in a strange paved room, and disguise costs her the two seconds between fast and heavy with no library underneath to catch her. The hafter, because a found pool is a seat: she is stationary, the grey ring advertises exactly where, and he closes with no count and no line and takes the seat off her. The bookhand, who never comes near her — she has the district's channels, basins and gate-work in the record, and shuts a gate two streets away without ever standing on the ground; the walker feels the pressure fall four seconds before the working starts to die, and four seconds is not enough to move.

Socially. Hired to site wells, price ground before a purchase, find out whether the neighbour's new basin is quietly taking what used to come to your sill, and say how long a body has been in a room. Skilled, well-paid, unglamorous, and there is a queue for the good ones. Paid by the walk — a fee on the day and a cut if water comes, half up front, because half the answers are no and nobody likes paying for no. Not hired by anyone who needs the answer in a form they can show to somebody else, and there is a whole class of client who pays her privately and then commissions a proper survey to say the same thing in writing. They come to it as hired lads and girls on survey crews, at fourteen, on a wage, and spend two years covering the same fields until those fields are in library — which is not sensitivity, it is forty acres learned the way anybody learns a stair in the dark. Plenty of walkers read and write and keep field notes; some don't, the same as any trade. The other trades call them feelers, which started as an insult about method and has softened to about the level of chippy: a walker will use it about herself and take offence if a stock-caster uses it first.

Magnifying. Does not carry accelerant, because she works short off found pools with her hip a foot from her hands and accelerant is what a short working takes first. Owns the assay instead: hand her a sack and she will tell you in four seconds that it has been cut, though not with what, and every serious buyer of powder in the country knows a walker who will price a lot for a fee. Does not own fire; she owns the room's — she knows before anyone that the granary floor is four inches of dust, that the lime store shares a wall, that the resin has soaked the boards, and she can lay a boundary there and set there. Owns the runner on ground she has walked and nowhere else: she has covered the track and knows where the dense things sit, so hers arrives where she said it would, and off her ground the drag finds her own crew. Owns the aimed reach outright, and it is hers alone in this set — she sets short on purpose having already decided what the working will find, because she knows the cistern is behind the left wall, the lime store is under the stair, and the trough is eleven feet out the door. Everyone else banks a mass and hopes; she names a source she cannot see. Sites deep extraction and does not do it: she can say a district has been drawn hard, in the same four words the trade uses for a man — slack, thinning, hardening, failure — and so can the walker on the other side of the argument who says it has not, and one of them is wrong, and both are being paid.

What a lifetime does. Overdraw eats what was under load, and hers is palms, fingertips, the heel of the working hand, both knees, the braced forearms. Slack: hands that shake held open, a stride short by half a foot in the last hour. Thinning: always the flat hand, never the steering one — the muscle at the base of the thumb goes and the fingertips go glassy and smooth, and the fine end of her hands goes with them, so she loses the discriminations that were never the job and keeps the coarse ones that were. Hardening: grey-white at the nail beds, in the palm crease, over the kneecap, dense and cold and gritty, and the part sounds wrong when tapped. Walkers tap their own knuckles on a table without noticing; it is the trade's tell and nobody in the trade mentions it. Failure: a hand dead to feel, a knee that will not take weight. An old walker works off the back of the wrist and the inside of the forearm, kneels on one side only, and can still find a pool because pools are enormous — she has simply lost the ability to tell a filled floor from a true one on the first placement, so she takes four. The ones who stop fighting site wells. The ones who don't, don't get old.

The internal split. Stayers live and die on one ground, hold forty acres the way a foreman holds his own yard, own the only genuinely chained library made entirely of one place, and are the best caster within four miles and useless the moment they cross the river. Hire-walkers go ground to ground for money, never own a large working, never own known ground, and are the only people alive who can price land they have never stood on — and the stayers think they have sold the one advantage the trade has, while the hire-walkers think the stayers are farmers with cold hands.

Scene. The stair was over a vault and she had it three treads up: the ground went hollow under her palms, like a plank with nobody on the far end. She backed off it, staked the cord across the landing behind her, put both hands flat on the flags twenty feet from the doorway and found the thread of pool off to the left, thin as a wet rag. The grey started coming out from her knees. Somebody shut a gate upstream while she was drawing, and she felt the pressure fall the way a tap goes — four seconds of warning and nowhere to go with them. So she solved the fourth tread short on purpose and took her hands off it. Half-set came up out of the stair, dull and colourless and warm as a palm, and the first man down came out of his boot with the sole gone.

·THE COUNTER GRAPH

Twelve edges, five nodes, every school beating at least two and beaten by at least two. Three pairs run both ways, conditioned on terrain — that is deliberate: the home-ground rule is canon, so the same two schools trade places when the room changes. Nobody is unbeaten. Nobody is a designated victim.

Pinch hand is beaten by — - the hafter, because she cannot pour with her hands full and he has no reload; the pour is one per exchange and she is defenceless through it, so he forces exchanges faster than she can restock her own hand. - the carrier, in a room neither prepared, because nine kilos of loose graded stock rides on the outside of her body; a palm-ring closed on the harness strap solves the harness in under a second and the board goes on the floor. - the ground-walker, on ground the walker has walked, because the walker aims the reach: she sets short having already decided what the working finds, and the most out-of-rest matter in the room is the pinch hand's chest.

Hafter is beaten by — - the carrier, at contact range, because she is the only school whose fast tier works the object in your hands: steel is deep in her library, she closes the ring on the rod, and he is a man with a heavy sleeve. - the bookhand, because she picks the building: she knows which pillar was patched, which flight was rebuilt in rolled steel, which fittings were re-hung when the supplier changed — and he works at the end of a tool, from assumption, at full chained speed.

Carrier is beaten by — - the ground-walker, because a walker's routine output is half-set and the carrier's contact surface is her hand; half-set across the routes into a room does not slow the carry down, it excludes her. - the pinch hand, when the pinch hand was in the building first: her own plate on the doorframe, her own billets on the landing. Substitution beats library, the carry is the most library-dependent school alive, and a chain cannot be aborted.

Bookhand is beaten by — - the pinch hand, who is a walking substitution and never has to fight her at all — she only has to have been in the building before her. - the carrier, who removes the building from the fight: the bookhand's speed is route, and inside a boundary the size of two people there is no next stanchion to walk to, and she cannot work a person.

Ground-walker is beaten by — - the pinch hand, on ground neither has met, because the walker has no fast tier off her own ground and no library underneath to catch a dazzle-plate, while the pinch hand's recognition came in on her back. - the hafter, because a found pool is a seat: she is stationary, the frost ring says exactly where, and he closes with no count and no line. - the bookhand, who never approaches: she has the district's channels and gate-work in the record and shuts a gate two streets away. A pool is denied upstream, and the walker's four seconds of warning are not enough to move.

Reciprocal pairs, and what flips them: pinch↔carrier flips on who prepared the room; pinch↔walker flips on whose ground it is; carrier↔... none. Out-degrees: pinch 3, carrier 3, hafter 2, bookhand 2, walker 2. In-degrees: walker 3, pinch 3, carrier 2, hafter 2, bookhand 2.

·WHAT THIS LEAVES OPEN FOR LATER GATES

  • The seating trade — who does implants, what eleven years of payment looks like, and whether a player can buy one.
  • Pool ownership — sills, gates, basins and who holds them; established here only as the reason nobody wants a carrier near their water.
  • The Table's editions — the orthodox/amender quarrel needs a publishing body, or a stated absence of one.
  • The crack in the law — four schools now walk up to name your source, and the dry men walk past it. One faction should own that argument, not five.
  • Relics — named as locked surplus in canon, used by nobody here; still unclaimed.
  • The record's forgers — the bookhand's library is the only one that can be poisoned months in advance, which implies people who do that for a living.
  • Half-set as a commodity — walkers manufacture it, carriers bank it, hafters die of it; nobody yet sells it.
  • What is at the bottom of a deep extraction — nobody has read it, and three schools are drilling toward it.

1. WHICH ARE PLAYABLE — four.

Pinch hand. Moment to moment: thumb-counting a physical pocket board, deciding what leaves your body, and the constant tension of do I pour now or keep my hands free. Best onboarding in the set — a loadout screen made of nicks, and no permanent loss. Ship her.

Carrier. Moment to moment: walk into contact, palm on the thing, and watch a mark move up your own arm at the instant you spend. Every fight is a question about how much of your remaining forearm this is worth. The most distinct feel in the game and the only one whose resource is a map that shrinks.

Hafter. Moment to moment: pure tempo — no counting, no lines, thumb walking bores while you press forward. Easiest to make feel good with a controller, and the only school with a real disarm state (wet strap over the seam and you are a civilian). Also the highest risk of being too clean; if the seat's fragility doesn't land, he is a fighting game character in a mining setting.

Bookhand. Moment to moment: recon as a mechanic — you spend the night before in the salvage yard putting a district into memory, then walk the level at pace touching four things. Playable only if levels reward pre-study; if the game is procedural or arrival-driven, she is a designer's promise you cannot keep, and she should drop to enemy-only.

2. ENEMY-ONLY — the ground-walker.

Her power is twenty years on forty acres, which a travelling protagonist can never accumulate and a player will never wait for. As an enemy she buys three things nothing else does: a fortress — a stayer who cannot be beaten where she lives and is irrelevant a mile away, which turns a boss fight into a decision about whether to go there at all; a weather event — the only silhouette that makes the floor itself hostile, so a room becomes the enemy; and the aimed reach, which is the setting's worst accident used deliberately, and it should be something done to the player before it is ever a tool. Keep hire-walkers as hireable NPCs so players can borrow the fantasy without owning it.

3. WHICH ONE THE PROTAGONIST STARTS IN.

Candidate A — the pinch hand. Opening hours: pockets, weight, a pre-fight repack, and a world you learn by milling it. Fights are inventory management with fire in them. The arc is supply: you get better by knowing more families and carrying less of them, and the game turns on the day the board goes in a river. Low emotional cost, high systems teaching, travels anywhere. It is the safe start and the weaker story.

Candidate B — the carrier. Opening hours: you have nothing and you can do it right now. The first ten hours are the most powerful the player will ever feel — catch a blade bare-handed and hand it back hollow — and the mark moves a thumb's width every time, on screen, at the moment you spend. The arc is the shrinking map: forearm to forearm to thigh to back, four sites in a lifetime, and the collarbone as a line the player can choose to cross. Power is fun and then it is terrible, in the same limb, on a gauge.

Recommendation: start in the carry. It is the only school where the game's own thesis is the health bar, and the pinch hand is the perfect second school — the thing you pick up when you finally understand why nobody should have to be the channel.

4. DECISIONS NOT ON THAT LIST THAT ARE EXPENSIVE TO REVERSE.

  • Paper is an index, not a library. If a later gate lets the record enter library directly, every school's ceiling moves and the bookhand stops being a class.
  • Irreversibility is a promise. If hardening can ever be healed — a relic, a surgeon, a late-game item — the carry has no arc and the whole overdraw vocabulary goes soft. Decide now, not at Gate 6.
  • Topology is fixed per character, or it isn't. Whether a player can be seated, buy a flask, or squat on a pool decides if schools are classes or loadouts. Everything above assumes classes.
  • The aimed reach belongs to one school. It is assigned to the walker. If a second school gets it, the crack in the law is spent before Gate 3 opens.
