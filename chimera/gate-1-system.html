Solve and Set

Matter is grain · the charge is the hold · zero coinages spent

Matter is grain: fine particles in families that never become one another. Every working is two motions — SOLVE parts the hold that keeps an arrangement together, SET re-ties it into a new one. Two separate things must be true before a working can happen, and the trade names them separately because confusing them is the classic apprentice error. You must have the substance: the actual families, in the actual proportion, inside the boundary you laid. And you must have the sap: the surplus charge that pays for parting the hold. Substance comes out of the world and is found by touch. Sap comes out of ground that has had time to work on matter forced out of rest, and it is dug for, carried, stolen, channelled and fought over. A working that has both does what you told it. A working that is short does not stop and ask. It finishes out of whatever is nearest that will serve — and there is always something nearest.

1The Grounding

Grain. All matter is particles, too fine to see, and they come in a limited number of families. A family is a kind of grain. Families never become one another: no working has ever turned lead into gold, water into iron, or bone into anything but bone-stuff and water. Every working is a rearrangement of what was already in the room.

The Table of families is published, disputed, and incomplete. Practitioners argue about it in print and in taprooms. Nobody has the whole list, several entries on it are certainly two families filed as one, and a working practitioner meets an unfiled family perhaps twice in a career. This is why comprehension is the trade's progression and memorisation is not.

The trade's literacy is empirical. It knows matter is divisible, that it behaves by family, that families combine in fixed proportions and can be separated back out. It has no account of what a particle is made of and does not pretend to one. Purity, grade, proportion, provenance: those are the questions a professional asks, and they are enough.

The hold. Grain does not lie loose. It is held — bound into arrangement, and the arrangement is what makes a thing the thing. Brass is held; so is bone, so is river water, so is the air. A working is unbinding and rebinding. SOLVE parts the hold. SET re-ties it.

The charge is the hold, and it is called sap. It sits in every particle because everything is held. Almost none of it is spendable. In matter at rest the entire charge is doing structural work; drawing on it would be drawing on the wall you are standing on, and the wall does not permit it. Only matter forced out of rest carries surplus — charge above what its present arrangement needs. Surplus is the only sap anyone has ever used. Where matter gets forced out of rest, and how the surplus is moved before it re-holds, is Section 4.

A working is two problems at once. Do I have the stuff? and do I have the charge to part it? They fail differently, they are supplied differently, and a caster who is rich in one and poor in the other is a specific and recognisable kind of dangerous.

The worked line

A player must be able to reason from this unaided. The canonical demonstration, the one an apprentice is walked through in the first week:

"Brass is copper and zinc, seven to three. So: wire, zinc powder, any shape of brass I like. Not steel — there is no iron in brass. Ask anyway and the working goes and finds iron: the nails in that door, the buckle on my belt. Ask short of lime or water, and the nearest source is the man standing behind me."

Read that as arithmetic, because that is what it is. A working is an inventory. You name a product, and the product has a composition; you lay a boundary, and inside that boundary is what the working will take. If what is inside matches what the product needs, you get the product. If it does not, the working does not abandon the job — it completes it out of what is actually available, and the four ways that goes are Section 5.

Everything a player needs to improvise follows from three facts and no others:

  1. Families never convert. You cannot make what you did not bring a family of.
  2. Proportion is real and is arithmetic. Seven to three means seven to three. Short of zinc is short of zinc, and the shortfall does not average out.
  3. Nothing is discarded and nothing is invented. A working takes what it needs from inside the line if it can and from outside the line if it cannot.

The only layer is this one. There is no far side, no celestial tier, no spirits, nothing that listens, nothing that bargains. A working is a physical operation on physical matter, performed badly or well.

2The Two Strokes

Every working is two motions, and they are opposites. An apprentice who cannot feel the difference in his hands by the end of the first afternoon should be sent back to the forge.

Solve works against the hold. Parting an arrangement while the thing is still standing there is the hard act. That is why solve takes almost all the sap, why it is the rare skill, and why it fails by simply not happening.

Set works with the hold. Loosened matter is already trying to re-tie; you are not building the hold, you are steering one that is arriving anyway. That is why set is cheap, why most trained hands can manage it, and why it does not fail so much as go wrong.

A failed solve leaves you poorer. A failed set leaves you a thing.

SOLVE

Requires: unbroken contact — skin, or a tool you have read through — for the whole stroke. The read runs through touch; break the touch and the stroke is over. Costs: effectively all of the sap. Cost scales with how much you open and how tightly it is held. Recognising the material buys you seconds, not sap: a library entry makes a heavy working fast, it does not make it cheap. Takes: 0.2–0.4 s on recognised material. On unfamiliar material, add the read — two seconds to forty, depending on acuity (Section 3). Nine-tenths of a slow working is the reading. Interrupted: nothing loosens and the sap is spent. Mode 1, and it is the lucky outcome: the caster is poorer and everyone is alive. Skilled vs desperate: the skilled hand opens the smallest volume that will do the job and no more. The desperate one opens everything she is touching. A desperate solve is not weaker in the moment; it is larger, and everything downstream of a large solve is worse.

SET

Requires: the matter still open, and a boundary. Set can be carried through the boundary rather than through the skin — the line is your handle once the touch has done its work. Costs: roughly a tenth of the solve; up to a third for fine steering into an unfamiliar shape. Takes: 0.2–0.5 s, but a two-stroke working must land inside the open window, and the window shrinks with volume. A fist of brass gives you about two seconds. A door gives you well under one. Large workings cannot be taken in two strokes; they must be chained. Interrupted: the matter does not go back. It re-ties on its own terms — half-set, standing, persistent, and still there when everyone has walked away (Section 5, mode 2). Failure character: set always completes. It has no clean miss. Every fault in a set is a delivered object — warped, half-tied, or made out of something that was not offered.

The window and the clock run opposite ways, and that is the point. The open window is how long the matter will still take a shape. The re-hold is how long it takes to stop being dangerous — and that runs the other way: a palm-sized patch re-holds in about ninety seconds, a door's worth in twenty minutes. Big volumes lose steerability fastest and stay open longest. That is exactly what makes them the worst thing to abandon.

The Boundary

The boundary is a closed run of contact around the material: chalk, wire, wet cord, a groove scored in the flagstone, the rim of a crucible, a belt laid in a circle, or a caster's own two hands and a boot. It is physical. It can be drawn and it can be broken. When the stroke runs, the line shows — a dry, dust-fine mark along its whole run, across skin as readily as across stone.

The boundary declares. It does not contain. What is inside the line is what the working will take first. It is not a wall; a sound line does not stop a short working going outside for the rest (Section 5). Anything inside the line is a source, whether or not the caster meant it to be — including a person standing in it.

A flat palm on a wall is a closed run: the rim of contact is the line, and it encloses very little. That is why FAST workings are small. A working with no closed run at all does not fire; the solve does not take, the sap is spent, and the trade files that under mode 1 along with everything else that comes to nothing.

The professional habit follows: boundary first, contents named second, contact third, stroke last. Casters who work in company draw wide and close the line where they can see it. Casters who work alone get careless, and the trade knows exactly what careless looks like.

Chaining

Chaining is the fusion of the two strokes into one gesture: the set begins before the solve has finished resolving, and there is no gap between them. Duration varies with volume — a fitting is a second, a door leaf is several — but the shape is the same, one continuous motion, grip, part, close.

The price is exact. A chained gesture sets against your recognition rather than against your read, and it cannot be aborted once begun. You have committed to what you believed the material was. If the fittings have been swapped, if the plate is a facing over something else, the chained working does not stop and ask; it sets wrong, or it reaches. A chained working also spends roughly a quarter more sap than the same working taken in two strokes, because nothing was measured.

The consequence, stated out loud: a caster cannot take a large working on unfamiliar material at all. Large means chained; chained means from library. She can read the thing first, at leisure, and then chain it — that is what PREPARED is for. What she cannot do is meet a door-sized problem made of something she has never touched and solve it in a fight.

Two strokes taken separately can be abandoned in the gap. That gap is the only abort a caster has, and speed is bought by giving it up.

Who Sets, and Who Solves

Set is the follow-through most trained people can manage. This has a shape, and the shape is a crew.

A foundry floor has one or two solvers and a dozen hands who can steer a set. An orderly in a field hospital cannot open a wound, but he can carry the set the surgeon opened, and does, all night. Masons work the same way: one man parts the joint, three close it. The solver is the scarce, expensive, insured member of the crew, and everyone else on the floor is watching her hands and waiting to take the other end.

In a fight this means a caster with two competent hands beside her is not one caster, she is three — she opens and they steer, and each of them can carry a set she has no time to finish. It also means the counterplay is obvious to any sergeant: take the solver, and the setters are three people with chalk.

Interruption, and Why the Answer Is Not "Hit Them"

A caster is interrupted three ways: break the contact (the read runs through touch), break the boundary (scuff the chalk, kick the wire out of true, throw sand or water across the line), or take the substance out from inside the line.

Timing is the whole of it. Break a caster during the read or the solve and you get mode 1 — nothing loosens, her sap is gone, and you have won cleanly for the cost of a shove. Break her during the set and the matter is already open; it will re-tie regardless of what you do to her. You get half-set matter standing where she was, or a set that goes looking for its shortfall — and you are a man standing close, made largely of water and lime.

So the drill taught to soldiers is not hit the caster. It is hit the caster early or hold and let it land — the haze is rising, take her; the haze is falling, get off the line. Non-casters who last are the ones who learned to read the haze, foul a chalk line from six feet with a boot-load of grit, and know that the safest moment to tackle a caster is before her hand touches anything at all.

3Axis One: Substance — the Read

Every working is a demand for substance. The demand is met from inside the boundary the caster lays, or from outside it. The read is how she finds out what she is standing in. It is the first half of every working and the half that decides whether the second half kills someone she likes.

What the read is

Contact, then resolution. She puts skin on the thing — palm, fingertips, the back of a knuckle, the sole of a bare foot on a floor — or a tool she has already read through, and for as long as she holds contact the material resolves under her hand like sediment dropping out of stirred water. It is not sight and it is not thought. Practitioners describe it as pressure with a shape.

It is a real perceptual capacity, and everybody has some. It is not hereditary, no bloodline owns it, nobody is tested for it and shut out at twelve. The extremes are genuinely far apart, but the low end is a slope, not a wall.

A completed read returns four things, always in this order, because that is the order they resolve:

  1. Families — which are present, coarse first, trace last.
  2. Proportion — how much of each, by part. Copper and zinc, seven to three.
  3. Depth — how far past the surface the reading holds true, and where it stops.
  4. State — at rest or forced out of rest; worked before or never worked; half-set.

State is the one novices skip and the one that gets them hurt.

The read tells you what is in the boundary you laid. It does not lay the boundary — your hands do that.

Acuity is what a single touch gives you: resolution, depth, and speed. Largely natal, widely distributed, improves by small increments over years, and has a hard personal ceiling. It is degraded permanently by overdraw to the hands and by a reach that runs through her own tissue.

Library is what she has already read and retained. Purely earned, additive, no ceiling. It cannot be taken from her — but it can be made wrong, which is worse than losing it.

Recognition is not a read. A material already in library resolves on first impression, under a quarter-second, at any acuity, because she is not measuring it — she is matching it. This is the whole of the FAST tier: recognising drops a working from HEAVY to FAST, because the reading time is gone, not because the working is easier.

A thin-acuity practitioner with two hundred materials in library out-fights a strong-acuity practitioner in a room full of things neither has met, and she does it on speed. Two casters can be equally dangerous by opposite routes. Acuity is not a power stat, and the low-acuity grinder is genuinely viable — that is what working forever buys.

The ceiling, from inside. It does not feel like a wall. It feels like pressing on a closed door with a hand already flat against it: more attention, more time, more will, and the material gives back exactly what it gave back a moment ago. Practitioners who have hit theirs stop pressing within a year or two and become library people — they carry sample cases, they cut into things, they ask what the fittings are made of. The ones who keep pressing get headaches, nosebleeds, and no additional resolution. There is no technique that raises it. Everyone worth anything in the trade knows where their edge is and works up against it without touching it.

Home ground is known ground, not rich ground. A caster who walked that corridor yesterday fights differently in it today, because half the room is already in library and half the room is therefore FAST.

Being wrong

The read is honest about what her hand is on. It is not honest about what her hand is on top of. The read resolves from the surface down and stops at the first seam it meets. A continuous surface family reads as the whole body of the thing unless she deliberately drives the read past the seam — which costs her a second look and the seconds to take it. Thickness is not the deceit. The seam is.

  • Plating and tinning. Silver wash over copper, tin over iron, pewter over lead.
  • Facing and cladding. A hand's breadth of dressed stone over rubble and mortar fill. Reads as ashlar to anyone who does not read deeper than the ashlar goes.
  • Fills and cores. A hollow rail packed with sand. A lead core in an iron boss.
  • Coatings. Pitch, tallow, beeswax, varnish, whitewash, grease, dried blood. These blur depth and add a family that was not structurally there.
  • Contamination. A trace family below her resolution — salt in the water, sulphur in the coal, two parts in a hundred of something in the iron. Present enough to take part in the set, too small for a thin read to see.
  • Previously worked matter. Set material reads tighter than its proportion suggests. Half-set matter reads loose, and is the single most misread state in the world; people who do not know the signature take it for soft stone.
  • Deliberate dressing. Dazzle-plate: sheet stock hammered thin, wired or glued over the real material specifically to be read.

Downstream, exactly. Solve acts on the material as read, not as true, and set delivers the demand as named. These fail at different points, and the distinction is the whole of it:

  • A mis-read of the material you are solving fails the solve. Call it brass when it is bronze, and the working addresses a material that is not there. Nothing loosens. Mode 1.
  • A shortfall in the substance the set demands reaches or warps. Demand silver from a plated fitting and there is a little silver present, so the working completes out of what is there — or goes outside for the rest. Which one is Section 5, and it is decided by what is in range, not by how good your line is.

Counterplay

Disguise beats acuity. It attacks the first hand-on. A soldier greases his buckles and blackens his fittings; a careful householder lime-washes the door furniture and paints the hinges; an armourer tins the outside of a cheap boss and pitches the inside of the shield. None of this requires talent, and all of it costs a caster seconds she does not have. Against a strong reader, disguise buys about two seconds — which is the difference between a FAST working and a HEAVY one, and a HEAVY one is a caster standing still where you can reach her.

Substitution beats library. It attacks the match. Swap the brass hinge pins for iron. Change the barracks fittings between the survey and the fight. Run glazed clay where the pipe was lead. Salt good stock with a tenth part of something else so the recognition is off by exactly the margin that matters. The material is not hidden — it is different, underneath a surface that still reads familiar.

This is why a veteran in a substituted room is worse off than a novice in it. The novice reads everything, slowly, and gets the truth. The veteran recognises, skips the resolve entirely, and works from memory at full speed, confidently, on a material that is no longer there. Once she learns the room is dressed she must suppress recognition and verify by hand, and that is slower than her own novice self, because she has to read the thing and disprove what she already knows. Substitution is how you take a caster's home ground away without setting foot in it.

One problem, three rooms

The problem: something is coming through that door, and she needs it not to.

In a forge. Bar stock on the rack, recognised on contact — wrought iron, in library, under a quarter-second. She lays her left palm flat on the door leaf across the strap hinge and pins the bar against the jamb with her right, both hands inside the boundary so that strap, nails and bar are one volume. She names the bar aloud as her source, solves the strap and the jamb nails loose, and sets the whole demand into a continuous band running leaf to stone. A HEAVY: she is stationary, both hands committed, about four seconds, chained throughout. The bar is gone; the rack is one bar lighter and everyone will notice. Sap off the forge floor, which is quench-shocked and worked and the richest ground in the building.

In a flooded cellar. Knee-deep water, brick barrel vault, lime mortar in the joints. She wants a plug in the doorway; lime and water make one. She works the joints of the near wall, the one she does not need, digging two fingers into the mortar while laying her other hand flat on the water so the water is unambiguously inside the boundary and unambiguously named. Both materials are in anyone's library, so she can chain it: solve the lime out of thirty feet of joint, set it with the water into the door aperture, and it goes off hard in under a minute. The near wall will come down within the hour and she must not be under it when it does. Note where she is standing: in a flooded cellar the caster is inside her own source volume. Ask short of lime here and the nearest lime is her own legs.

In a bare stone cell. No metal, no fittings, no water but her own, poor ground and a wooden door. She cannot add anything, so she does not add: she redistributes. She has time, so she takes the read properly first — fibre, water, the ash families in the grain — and only then puts both palms flat on the leaf, thumbs hooked over the edge, and chains it: solving the centre panels and setting them into the edges and the jamb until the door is one continuous piece with the frame. The demand for water runs a little short and she names her own mouth rather than let it reach for the guard outside; she will not speak clearly for a day. And the arithmetic holds against her: a door leaf contains exactly one door leaf's worth of wood, so the edges are welded and the centre is now thin as a dinner plate. She has bought minutes, not hours, and anyone with a boot knows it.

4Axis Two: Sap

What sap is

Sap is the hold itself: the charge that sits in every particle because everything is held. Almost none of it can be spent. In matter at rest the entire charge is doing structural work — it is what makes the thing the thing. Only matter forced out of rest carries surplus: charge above what its present arrangement needs. Surplus is the only sap anyone has ever used.

The earth as refinery

A living body is matter that never settles. It is heated, worked, torn and rebuilt every hour it is alive, and it spends its surplus as fast as it makes it. At death that stops, and the accumulated unrest leaves the body into whatever the body is touching.

It leaves into the ground, and it is not sap when it arrives. Raw unrest out of a fresh body cannot be worked. A caster standing over a corpse has nothing. Death is not magic and sap is not a soul; a body is simply the richest deposit of not-yet-settled matter there is.

The ground's work is to let that unrest settle slowly enough that the surplus can be drawn off before it re-holds. The strata refine. They do not store — what stores is a pool, and a pool is downstream of the refining, not the same thing as it.

Contact is the whole of it. A body on flagstone, on a ship's deck, or in a lead-lined coffin gives nothing to any pool anywhere. Burial custom in every country in the world is an argument about this and everyone knows it. (What the body itself does over those nine days is Section 8.)

A battlefield is the best ground in the country, and everyone in the trade knows it. The trade arrives on day two with mattocks and sinking-rods and cuts its channels across a field that has not yielded anything yet, so that the run is ready when it does. Peak is a fortnight out. Nobody involved considers this remarkable; it is when the work is done.

Other matter forced out of rest carries surplus too. Fault lines and shock ground. Quench pits behind any foundry — the shot at the bottom is worth collecting and every foundry sells it. Crushing floors. Spoil from previously worked ground. All of it thin, none of it enough to fight a war on, all of it enough to keep a country's ordinary work running.

Hydraulics

Sap sinks and it runs. It moves through permeable strata the way water does, a few yards a day, and gathers wherever the ground beneath is tight — a clay lens, a rock shelf, an old floor. That gathering is a pool. Pools have a depth, a rough volume, a rate of inflow, and a sill: the level above which they spill and run on downhill to the next pool.

Sap that reaches a pool is held there and does not re-hold. That is what makes a pool a pool, and it is what makes a lined basin worth the digging. The eight-week clock is the settling of raw unrest in open ground; get it into a pool inside the window and it keeps.

The real work of the trade is hydraulic. You cut channels to bring a pool to where you want it — a cut channel runs sap far faster than strata will, the length of a field in a day, which is the entire reason for cutting one. You connect one pool to another. You cut a basin — a lined pit, tight-floored, sunk across the run — to gather and hold what would otherwise have gone somewhere else.

A basin cut upstream of another man's works is an act of war, and a slow one. His pool does not fail that week. It thins over a season, and by the time the drawing floor can prove it, the season's sap is sitting in someone else's basin and the channel that stole it has grass on it.

The characteristic crime is quieter: a bore run in from your land to a throat below another man's sill, drawing off the bottom of his pool where the level never shows it. It is not theft of a thing. It is theft of a rate. It is answered, when it is answered, with shovels.

Upstream and downstream are relationships between people, not places on a map — and they are parish-scale by design. Sap does not travel countries. Your enemy in this is your neighbour, the man whose field touches yours, and that is deliberate: it puts the whole politics of the resource inside walking distance and inside a grudge.

Tapping, and why industry exists

A tap is a bored shaft to a pool's depth with a set-stone throat — the lined mouth the sap comes up through — and a gate above it. Opening a gate does nothing by itself; sap will not climb. You have to give it somewhere to go, and that means matter already out of rest.

A caster at work is herself matter out of rest, so a working is its own extractor. She can draw off a pool she is in contact with — a boot flat on the stone, a palm on the floor — as fast as she can work. What she cannot do with her hands is fill a flask. Decanting bulk sap into carriers is a machine's job, and that is what the industry is for.

The extractor is that machine: a hammer-train or a rocking bed, a beam engine driving a mass of graded shot or crushed spar — the white gangue off any lead workings — in permanent motion over the throat. Moving matter takes surplus off the ground beneath it, and the charged mass is bled into flasks on a two-minute cycle. It is an eight-hour shift, deafening, filthy with spar dust, and cold: a live drawing floor stays cold enough that the men work in gloves in August. Extractor men have white hands to the second knuckle, permanently, and are paid accordingly.

Relics

Relics are old-world objects set by better hands than anyone living has. Their surplus sits behind a small lock in the setting itself, so a bare touch bleeds them — a few seconds of full draw, then nothing until the lock recovers, which takes days.

And the lock is what wears. Every bleed takes a little of it, permanently: the recovery lengthens, the draw shortens, and after enough hands a relic is an old object that does nothing. Nobody alive can make another. Nobody alive fully understands how they were set, and the ones who claim to are selling something. A relic spent is a piece of the old world gone, and the trade counts the ones still working.

The three topologies

All three are in play at once, and they must fight differently.

Pool is artillery. She does not move, everyone knows exactly where she is, and she can throw HEAVY workings all afternoon. The fight is about the ground under her feet, and it is fought by everyone else in the room.

Flask is a magazine. Exact, portable, finite. She chooses which strokes are worth a flask, and the whole fight is arithmetic she does out loud, because she has to. The fight is about her supply, and it can be won before it starts.

Implant is a duellist. No count, no line, no ground: she chains strokes at a rate neither of the others can hold, for as long as her seat holds, and she cannot be cut off from her sap by anything an enemy does at range. The sap is inside the working with her, and the bargain for that is Section 7.

Running low, and running out

Drawing sap chills. The stages are the same in everyone, readable across a room, temporary, and they clear:

  1. Hands white to the second knuckle.
  2. Breath fogging in warm air.
  3. A fast fine tremor in the fingers — the flutter.
  4. Slurred consonants and a fixed stare. One stroke left, at most.

Running out has three outcomes and they are not the same event. The rule is stated once, in Section 7, because the third one is overdraw. In brief: too little sap thrown at a stroke and it simply fails to solve; sap exhausted after the solve and the caster stops, and the loosened matter stays loosened; sap exhausted and the caster pushes on anyway, and the working takes the difference out of her.

This is why casters count flasks aloud, and why a good one calls the last one.

Moving it, stealing it, cutting it

Sap is stored in a carrier, because charge sits in particles. A flask is a sealed measure of graded quartz shot or crushed spar, charged. Standing full, a flask loses about a twelfth of its charge a day — dead inside two months. Cored stone holds for months and is heavy, which is why supply lines exist and why they are worth ambushing. A holed flask vents; do not be near one.

Adulteration is mixing spent carrier through charged, or wetting it. Cut sap starts a working at full speed and stops partway — a three-quarter flask solves a beam and leaves the last hand's width half-set. The test is public and takes ten seconds: draw a bead in the shop and watch how far the frost climbs the flask. Frost short of the neck, walk away.

What the world runs on

Foundries set alloy true instead of hoping the melt was honest. Field surgeons solve bone shards out of a wound and set a fracture in forty seconds, and the lime comes from a jar on the tray, named aloud, because a surgeon who does not name her source has one standing right there. Masons set stone to stone with no mortar. Farmers break hardpan and solve salt out of a flooded field. Well-men clear a fouled well in an afternoon. Tanners, dyers, drainage crews, road gangs.

It is a trade. It has shifts, grades, and a very high rate of injury, and the men and women who do it talk about sap the way a millwright talks about head and fall: how much you have, how far it drops, and what happens to everyone downstream if you take too much.

5The Four Failure Modes

Four ways a working goes wrong. All four are rules. None of them roll dice. A journeyman can teach each one to an apprentice in a single sentence, and does, usually while pointing at something that has already gone wrong.

Which mode you get is decided by two questions the working asks and answers without you: did the hold part? and is everything I named actually here? Nothing else is consulted.

Solve took Substance Result
1 No — Fails to solve. Sap spent, nothing moves
2 Yes Set stroke interrupted or abandoned Half-set matter
3 Yes Short, and nothing in range holds enough of the missing family Sets wrong
4 Yes Short, and a source in range holds enough Reaches

Modes 3 and 4 are the same shortfall meeting two different rooms, and which one you get is mostly decided by which family you are short of. Zinc, silver, tin, iron in quantity — these are not lying about, and a working short of them usually finds nothing in range and warps. Water and lime are everywhere, in walls, in cisterns, in plaster, and in people. That is why the reach is the one everyone is afraid of, and why it is not the thing that happens every time.

1. It fails to solve

One breath: nothing loosens, the sap is gone anyway, and you have lost two seconds and learned something.

The solve does not part the hold. Three causes, all of them the caster's: the read was wrong (you called brass and it is bronze — no zinc in it, and the working is addressing a material that is not there); the read was too shallow (you read the plating and not the plate under it); or you did not put enough sap behind the stroke to part a hold that dense. Cold-worked steel, dense stone and anything a relic-grade hand set will eat a competent solve and sit there.

It looks like nothing. That is its signature. A brief bloom of frost-fine dust where the stroke landed, a smell like struck flint, and the material unchanged. The caster's hand comes away dry and slightly numb. Sap draws on the attempt, not the result — the stroke is paid for at the moment it is thrown.

It is the lucky one because the working never opened. Nothing was loosened, so nothing can go unset, warp, or go looking. You are poorer and slower and the fight is worse, and nobody behind you is now short a litre of water.

The clever use: deliberate failed solves are the standard field test. Throw a cheap stroke at an unfamiliar wall, spend a thimble of sap, and the failure tells you your read is wrong before you commit a heavy working with a person standing behind you. Cautious professionals do this constantly. The trade calls it a knock — he knocked on it first — and an armourer who has plated a door correctly is happy to let you try.

2. It solves but never sets

One breath: it's open and nothing shut it. Don't touch it.

This is the big one, and it is not an explosion. It is a persistent state.

What it is. Half-set matter is grain that has been unbound and left unbound. The families are all still present and still separate — no family has become another, that never happens — but nothing holds them in arrangement. It is not liquid, not gas, not dust. It behaves like extremely fine wet sand with no weight of its own: it holds a shape until touched, then slumps. It is body-temperature, always, whatever it was made from.

Causes. The caster is interrupted between strokes — struck, moved, tackled, deafened, dropped. Or the sap runs out after the solve and she stops (Section 7). Or she chose to solve and then had to abandon the working to save her own life, which is the commonest cause and the reason experienced casters throw fewer heavy strokes in melee than novices do.

Duration. It re-holds on its own, from the outside in, at a rate set by mass. A palm-sized patch in about ninety seconds. A door's worth in twenty minutes. A collapsed wall's worth can sit for a day and a half. What it becomes is ugly, weak and characteristic — grey, porous, crumbling, useless for anything structural. Every field in the country has patches of it. Builders call the re-held stuff slump and will not build on it.

Movement. It does not grow. A mass slumps and creeps along whatever it is already touching, a few feet from where it was made, and then no further. What it does instead is conduct: anything already-solved that touches it stays open longer, and a second caster can solve into an existing mass and enlarge it, which is how you get the big ones.

What it does to what touches it. It takes the hold out of the contact surface. A boot in half-set matter comes out with the sole gone — not dissolved, unbound, the leather grain sitting loose in the mass. Give it four or five seconds against skin and the skin goes with it; longer and it is into tissue, and the wound does not bleed properly because the vessel walls have come apart rather than been cut. Field hospitals treat half-set contact with immediate mechanical scraping and then wait. It is agonising and it is the only thing that works.

It is also a source, and a rich one. It is matter maximally out of rest with every family in it present and separate. A short working near a mass of it takes the mass before it takes anything else — including before it takes a person. It carries surplus, too, and a caster who wants that sap has to put a hand in it, which is why almost nobody does.

Cleanup. Sand, lime and time. You bank it, you rope it, you wait for it to re-hold, and then you break out the slump and cart it off. Town gangs do this. In a long war there are crews who do nothing else, and they are paid well and die of it slowly.

The clever use: it is terrain, and it is free once made. Solve a doorway, abandon the set, and you have twenty minutes of a door nobody can walk through — including you. Bank it into a channel and it flows where you put it. Throw a shield into a mass and drag it out to strip an enemy's plate to bare grain. And the professional's version: bank a mass beside you before you start working short. Every shortfall you throw feeds on it instead of on the people in the room. The best casters in the world leave half-set matter deliberately and are careful about which way the wind is going.

3. It sets wrong

One breath: enough sap, not enough substance, nothing in reach — so the working finishes out of what is actually in your hands, and gets it technically right.

The governing rule, stated once:

A short working sets the full named form at the proportion of the substance actually present, and makes up the missing volume with void.

It does not scale down. It does not substitute. It builds the thing you named, at the size you named, out of what is there — and the difference comes out as porosity, distributed evenly through the whole piece. The proportions between families that are present stay exactly as you named them. Nothing becomes anything else.

If you have 70% of the substance for a solid bar, you get a full-sized bar that is 30% void by volume, right through, and it takes about a third of the load. The percentages are for the workbench. In a fight the void is heard, seen and felt in the hand — it rings flat instead of bright, it takes a thumbnail, it is light when you lift it. Nobody does long division in a corridor.

Below roughly one part in ten, nothing coherent forms at all. The working throws the substance present as loose grain and the sap is gone. The trade calls that a dusting, and it is mode 1 with extra mess.

Three worked cases:

Brass rod. Named: a rod, brass, seven copper to three zinc, 100 measures. Present: 58.8 copper and 25.2 zinc — 84 measures, still exactly seven to three, because a third of the wire was tinned and got culled evenly out of both. Result: a full-length rod at 84% density, 16% void. It rings flat instead of bright. It bends under half the load it should take. It is a rod, and it is brass, and it is wrong.

Lime plaster patch. Named: a patch of two parts lime to one part sand, 60 measures, to seal a breach. Present: 24 lime, 12 sand — 36 measures, correct ratio, 60% of the volume, because the caster misjudged the breach. Result: a full-coverage patch at 60% density. It seals. It stops nothing. A shoulder goes through it.

Steel plate over a doorway. Named: 200 measures of plate. Present: an iron gate, a stack of tools — call it 150 measures of iron with the carbon in it. Result: a full doorway-sized plate, 25% void, evenly distributed. It holds against one man. It does not hold against three, and when it goes it goes all at once, in a sheet, because the void is even and so is the failure.

Note what this does to the caster's incentives: a short working produces something that looks finished. It is the failure mode you can walk away from without noticing, and the one that kills people three days later.

The clever use: deliberate voiding is a real technique. Set a barricade at 50% and it is half the weight and you can move it. Set a blade at 60% and it is fast and it snaps on the third parry, which is fine if you only need two. Set a wall thin on purpose and you know exactly where it will give and which way. Sappers do this professionally: build the plug short, and you own the breach's timing.

4. It reaches

One breath: short working, and something within reach has what it needs, so it goes and takes it.

The search. The working expands from its own centre and takes the first sufficient source it encounters. It is dumb, fast, and strictly geometric.

  • Range: roughly four metres for a fast stroke, out to twelve for a heavy one. Sap does not extend it; commitment does.
  • Order: nearest first, by straight-line distance from the working's centre. Not richest, not purest, not easiest. Line of sight is irrelevant — a wall between you and the source does not protect the source, and if the wall contains the family, the wall gets taken first.
  • Sufficiency: the working takes the whole shortfall from one source if that source holds enough, and moves outward to a second only if the first is exhausted.
  • What is not a source: anything the working cannot get purchase on. Air is not a source. There is water in the air and the working cannot have it; a hold that loose cannot be parted, and a read run into open air returns nothing at all. Neither is anything already fully spent. A source is a body of matter with a hold worth parting.
  • What it costs: nothing extra. The sap was committed when the stroke was thrown. A reach is free, which is exactly why it happens and exactly why it can be used.

From outside. A reach is visible and unambiguous, and that is deliberate. The boundary's dust-line jumps, in one frame, to a new object, and everything between goes untouched. There is a hard smell of wet stone. The source shows it: the door frame drops half a finger's depth of wood, the ground goes chalk-pale in a stripe, the man goes grey and sits down. Everyone in the room knows who paid. This is the single most important legibility rule in the system, and it is why the Law is muttered out loud rather than thought.

Aimed reaching. A reach is aimable, and it is the villain's method, not only an accident. The caster lays the boundary deliberately — running a hand or a tool along a line to define where the working believes its edge is, so that the nominated source is inside it and everything nearer is outside. It takes two to four seconds of contact, it is completely visible if you know the gesture, and it is precise to about a hand's width. That imprecision matters: laying a boundary to nominate the man on the left takes the man on the right too, if he is standing close.

To someone who knows what they are watching, an aimed reach reads as a caster who is not looking at their working. They are looking at a person. The gesture is slow, careful, and unmistakable, and there is a full two seconds in which to do something about it. That window is the counterplay.

By accident. The commoner case by far, and the one that generates the grief. A caster works short in a crowded room, calls the source correctly, and someone steps inside the boundary while the stroke is running. Or a foundry hand sets a heavy piece with the boundary laid at ten metres, correctly, and does not know a boy has come round the far side of the stack. The physics does not care about intent. The trade's habit of naming the source out loud exists because it is the only way to make people move.

What a reach takes out of a person: water and lime. Those are the two things a body holds in bulk. The arithmetic has to survive a player checking it, so:

Family In a grown adult (~70 kg) Usefully reachable
Water ~42 kg Several litres
Lime 2–3 kg, all of it the mineral in bone A few hundred grams, and it comes out of whatever bone is nearest the working
Iron ~4 grams About one nail. Nothing
Everything else trace Nothing

A working short of iron does not find iron in a person in any useful quantity — it finds the buckle on your belt or the nails in the door, and it takes those first anyway because they are nearer and denser. A working short of water or lime, in a room containing a person and no cistern, takes the person. That is the entire shape of the danger, and it is arithmetic, not malice.

The selection is dumb; the consequence is personal. Same taking, three people:

  • A field hand, twenty-two. Four litres, and the lime out of the long bone of the leg nearest the working. He goes down, cramps for a week, cannot hold food for two, and is back at work within the month with a headache that comes back in hot weather.
  • A woman in her seventies. The same four litres, the same lime, out of a hip that was standing closest. It does not survive losing it. She walks with a stick from that day and she never walks without one again.
  • A reader. The same taking. She recovers like anyone else — except that the read runs through tissue, and lime came out of the structures it runs through. Her acuity drops a step and stays down. She can still work. She will spend the rest of her career reading in three touches what she used to read in one.

The clever use: reaching is a resource, not only a hazard. A caster who knows the search is nearest-first can feed it — set a bar of the needed family at their own feet before the stroke, and the working takes the bar and never looks further. That is standard professional practice and it is what carriable substance is actually for. Offensively, a clever player fights in the right room: work short of lime with your back to a plaster wall and the wall pays. Work short of lime in a bare stone yard with a man behind you and you have made a decision, whether or not you meant to.

6Magnifying a Working

Everything above describes a working that takes what is in front of it. This section is how a caster makes one do more than its size suggests — and it is where the trade's ordinary industry and its worst atrocity turn out to be the same three techniques, pointed differently.

Accelerant

Sap is the hold, and solving costs sap because the hold resists. Some matter barely resists at all.

Accelerant is matter already strained past comfort and held together by very little: fine powder, spirits, quicklime, drying oils, resin dust, flour off a mill floor. Its hold is loose, so it parts for a fraction of what its mass suggests, and what it gives up on the way out does part of the stroke's work.

The rule, stated once: accelerant does not give a caster more sap. It lowers what the stroke costs. Same reserve, larger working. A hand of good powder inside the line turns a stroke she could not have afforded into one she can.

It is a trade good with grades, and the grading is honest work: how fine, how dry, how pure, how long since it was milled. Damp accelerant is worse than none — sap goes into driving the water off before anything else happens, and the caster has paid for a stroke she did not get. Cut accelerant, bulked with spent carrier or chalk, starts strong and quits partway. The test is the same one used on sap and takes as long: draw a bead in the shop and watch it.

And here is what it costs, which is not the same as what it is worth. Accelerant is matter maximally out of rest with its families present and separate. That is the exact description of the thing a short working takes first. A caster carrying accelerant is carrying the nearest source in the room, on her hip. Every shortfall thrown anywhere near her feeds on her pouches before it feeds on anybody's water and lime — which sounds like a mercy until you have watched a flask of spirits go into a stranger's working from six feet away.

Crews know this. Accelerant travels in the middle of a column and never on the solver, and a man who sets his powder down beside somebody else's work gets told about it once.

Fire

Nothing in this system produces fire as an effect. There is no fire family and there never will be. Two rules already stated produce it anyway, and the trade found this out early.

The set delivers heat. Every set, always — one hard snap and a breath of heat as the hold re-ties.

So: set an accelerant, and the stroke ignites what it has just made. The working is its own ignition source. A caster who lays her line across a doorway, names the spirits in her flask and the air inside the line, and sets, gets a sheet of flame standing in the doorway — out of two rules an apprentice learns in the first week.

It is not a spell and does not behave like one. She must have the accelerant; nobody sets fire out of a bare stone cell. She must have air, and air is the thinnest, most miserable thing in the world to read. And the set delivers its heat at the boundary, which is to say wherever she drew the line, which is to say she can be standing inside it. Casters who work fire are recognisable at a distance by their forearms, and by the fact that they draw wide.

Distance is a boundary problem, not a range problem. She does not throw the flame. She lays the line where she wants it, names what is inside it, and sets there. That is the whole of casting at range in this world, and it is the same operation as transposition: a working whose source is in one place and whose set is in another.

The runner

One breath: lay the line along a path, come up short on purpose, and let it eat its way there.

A boundary does not have to close around a thing. It can be laid as a run — drawn out across a floor, along a wall, down a street — and the working set to travel it.

Set that working short, deliberately, and it is short the whole way. Every foot of its path it reaches for the nearest source that will serve, takes it, and carries on. It arrives bigger than it left, and everything between the caster and the place it stopped has paid something toward it. The trade calls it a runner. The mark it leaves — a dust-fine track scoured along its whole path, with holes in it where it found something worth taking — is the drag.

Three things keep it from being the answer to everything, and all three follow from rules already stated.

It goes where the sources are, not where you point it. A reach takes the nearest sufficient source; a runner is a reach repeated, so it curves toward whatever is densest along its line — the iron gate, the water butt, the crowd. A caster aims the first few feet of it. After that she is steering a thing that is choosing.

There is no abort. A runner is chained by definition, and the gap between strokes is exactly what she gave up to get the range. Once the drag opens she is a spectator to it, and so is everyone else.

It is the most legible act on a battlefield. The drag is a line on the ground pointing back at the person who drew it and forward through everything that paid. There is no version of this that is not witnessed, and that is not a drawback the trade has found a way around.

The counter is the room, not the caster. A runner across bare wet flagstones is a scuff mark and a disappointment. A runner down a market street is what it is. This is why fighting withdraws to open ground when somebody in the field is known for it, why sappers clear a street rather than hold it, and why an experienced officer looks at the ground before she looks at the enemy.

Deep extraction

Lay the line to include the ground under your feet. Name what you want. Come up short.

The working goes down for it. This is how the country gets its ore, and it is done daily by crews who are not casters in any romantic sense — men on a drawing floor who lay a line, name iron, and stand well back while it comes up.

What it costs is that a reach takes the nearest sufficient source, and nobody has read what is down there. Sometimes iron. Sometimes the water table, and the district's wells go bad inside a week. Sometimes an old burial that no map showed. Sometimes the gallery crew working forty feet below, who are made largely of water and lime and are a great deal nearer than the seam.

The technique that hauls ore to the surface and the technique that kills the man behind you are one technique, pointed differently. The Law is most of what stands between them, and the Law is a sentence a tired person says under their breath.

The guardrail, stated once

Accelerant is supply, not progression. It swings a fight hard. It is bought, carried, stolen, cut and used up. It never makes anyone a better caster, and a campaign in which advancement means better powder has gone wrong.

A runner scales with the room, not with the caster. The same working thrown by the same person is nothing on a rock face and a massacre in a market. Nobody gets stronger. The ground gets worse.

7Overdraw

Overdraw is what happens when a working needs more hold than the surplus in front of it can supply and the caster does not stop. The shortfall is made up out of the nearest matter that is already forced out of rest — and tissue under strain has the loosest hold in the room.

Overdraw eats what you were using hardest. The world is not choosing. What you use hardest is what you are.

Running out of sap: the three-way branch, stated once

  • Not enough sap thrown at the stroke. The hold does not part. Mode 1: nothing loosens, the sap is spent, everyone is alive.
  • Sap exhausted after the solve, and the caster stops. The matter is open and nothing shuts it. Mode 2: half-set matter, standing where she was.
  • Sap exhausted, and the caster pushes through anyway. Overdraw. Everything below.

The third is a decision. It is always a decision. That is the whole of the design.

And it can take somebody else. The rule is the nearest matter already forced out of rest — if the man bracing the door has his shoulders under load and he is nearer to the working's centre than the caster's own legs, the working takes him. Crews are taught to step back off a heavy stroke and they are taught why. Casters who do not warn their crew get a reputation, and it is the specific reputation that gets a person left behind.

The stages

It is permanent. It arrives in four stages, in order, and never runs backwards. The trade uses the same four words for a man and for a district, on purpose.

Stage What it is Visible across a room
Slack The used tissue loses tone. Small permanent loss of capability, confined to one part A tremor that stays after the hands are still. Shortened stride on one side. A voice that runs out of breath at the end of a sentence
Thinning Visible wasting, asymmetric, confined to what was under load. One calf, one forearm, the throat The limb is plainly smaller than its pair. Skin over it is dry and takes a crease. Colleagues start standing on the good side
Hardening The wasted tissue is replaced by something that behaves like badly set matter: dense, cold, gritty under the thumb, chalk-coloured at the seams. Joints move through an arc and stop Grey-white bloom at the nail beds, the corner of the mouth, the crease of an elbow — efflorescence, the same as on a damp wall. The part sounds wrong when tapped. Gait audible before it is visible
Failure The part stops. A limb becomes dead weight; a throat makes no sound; a reader's hands resolve nothing at all. If it is a part you cannot live without, you die within the hour Obvious to a stranger at forty paces

A professional does not say he overdrew. She says what stage and where: "He's thin in the hands." "She's hardened at the right knee — she'll not be walking a channel again." "Slack, and he knows it, which is why he's holding the flask left-handed." The reading is diagnostic, not moral. Every crew has thin men on it.

There is no warning before the damage. Slack in your own hands is how a natural caster feels the line coming — which means the first stage is the warning, and the warning is already the injury. That is the bargain, it is understood, and every caster who works past a flask knows exactly what she is buying with.

Three cases, one working

A heavy set with too little surplus under it, taken past the line by the same margin in each.

  • The runner was moving when she pulled. It takes her calves and the long muscle of the thigh, both legs, unequal. At Thinning she loses roughly a fifth of her stride and all of her top speed; she can still walk fifteen miles in a day and will never sprint again. At Hardening the knee sets and she goes downstairs sideways for the rest of her life.
  • The reader was resolving a facing under her palms. It takes her hands and forearms, and it takes her acuity with them, because the read runs through tissue. At Thinning she loses depth first: she still resolves the surface and no longer resolves what is behind it. At Hardening she is finished as a reader of unfamiliar material — but her library is untouched, because library is not tissue. She becomes a caster who is fast on everything she already knows and useless on anything new. Several very dangerous old practitioners are exactly this.
  • The shouter was calling the stroke over a foundry floor. It takes the throat and the diaphragm. At Thinning his voice drops in pitch and loses all carry; at Hardening he has the bloom at the lip and takes four breaths to a sentence. He can still work. He can no longer run a floor, which was the whole of what he was.

The elective overdraw

Overdraw is the only source of hold that cannot be cut, stolen, gated, adulterated or read off you. Disarm a caster completely — empty her flasks, shut the gate upstream, put her on dead ground — and she still has one thing to spend, and both of you know it.

So professionals decide in advance. What overdraw takes is what was under load at the moment of the pull, and that is steerable. Take the stroke off a braced leg and it comes out of the leg. Take it with the off-hand flat and the good hand clear and it comes out of the off-hand. Sit down, put your back against the wall, take the whole load through your shoulders, and you have chosen your shoulders. A caster who has thought about it before the fight has already decided which part of herself she is willing to be worse at.

Slack in the off-hand is a trade a great many people make on purpose, once, for something worth it. The trade does not consider this brave. It considers it planning.

Implants under overdraw

Sap carried inside a person sits in an implant under constant low strain, which makes the implant the loosest hold the caster owns. Overdraw eats it before it eats tissue. This buys a real thing: an implanted caster can take one, sometimes two, overdraws that would have thinned a natural caster, and walk away with a spent implant and a grey seam instead of a hand that no longer closes.

Three costs, and only the first is obvious.

One: what actually goes is not the implant but its seat — the cuff of flesh the fitting is set into. Each overdraw eats a little more of the mounting. After the third, no seat will take a fitting again, and the caster is done being augmented, permanently, at whatever level of augmentation he happens to be at.

Two: the implant removes even the warning that Slack gives. An implanted caster feels nothing at all until the seat goes, and the seat goes all at once.

Three: the stages still run, they just run in the seam — bloom at the join, grey skin for two fingers' width around it, the joint cold to a stranger's touch. It is the easiest overdraw in the world to read across a room, which is why implanted casters wear sleeves.

Overdraw on the land

The same four stages, taken by pulling a pool past its surplus and into the hold of the ground itself.

Stage The district Duration
Slack Ground goes friable and dusty. Wells drop a foot. Mortar loosens; doors stop fitting their frames One to two seasons if left alone
Thinning Lime blooms white on brick and plaster. Water goes hard, then goes. Crops thin from the edges in. Every working done in thin ground reaches — the substance a set expects is simply not there Twenty to forty years. A generation
Hardening The ground sets into a dense pan a spade will not enter. Rain stands on it and burns off in the sun. Foundations crack upward. No pool re-forms; channels cut into it hold nothing Centuries. Only broken by shelling or quarrying, and then it is thin ground, not good ground
Dead A read of it returns nothing. No surplus, no refining. Workings there reach every time, and the only live source in a dead district is the people standing in it No known recovery. The oldest dead ground is exactly as dead as the day it was made

(Dead ground is ground that was killed. Ground that never had any is poor ground, and there is a great deal of it, and nobody fights over it.)

People do not leave thin districts, because the next district downstream belongs to somebody. What thin ground does to them it does quietly and by distribution: a working reaches, and it takes water and lime from whoever is nearest. A man out of a thin district is read by his teeth and the set of his hip, and the trade reads him at a glance and says nothing.

8The Dead

A body contains almost everything a person is made of. It is missing exactly the thing that mattered, and nothing comes back. Not the person, not by any technique, not once, not ever.

What happens instead is a documented phenomenon with a name and a clock. It is called the settling, and any competent professional can describe it flatly, because most of them have had to.

A living body is matter that never settles. When it dies, all that accumulated unrest goes into the ground, and it takes about nine days to go.

  • Days one to two: the vent is strongest. A fresh body is the densest thing in most rooms for water and lime, which means a short working near one takes it — it is nearer than the cistern and it holds enough on its own, so the search stops there. This is why nothing is worked in a house with a body in it, and why field crews clear the dead off ground before they set anything on it.
  • Day three or four: contraction. The body shifts on the slab — the arm draws up, the jaw opens, the trunk turns three or four inches. It is matter losing hold, in the same way a beam cracks as it dries.
  • Days five to eight: slackening. The surface reads wrong to the touch, proportions sliding under the hand.
  • Day nine: finished. The body is ordinary matter, and the ground beneath it has begun to make sap.

It does not stand. It does not respond to a name or a light or a hand. It has no preference and does not orient on anything. It does not injure anyone except by falling. It is not a creature, it has never been one, and no one in the trade has ever seriously argued otherwise.

The work is done by the layers-out: the burial trade in the towns, orderlies in a field hospital, field crews after a battle. Bodies are held three days behind a course of brick, strapped at chest and knee because they move, then put into open ground away from works, laid singly, weighted. Burning is bad practice and everyone competent says so — fire does not stop a settling, it disperses it, and now it vents wherever the ash lands, into a yard, a roof, a well.

The missing thing. A body is weighed and read before and after. Family by family, down to proportion, nothing has left. Every particle is present and accounted for, and the body now settles, and before it did not. The trade calls that discontinuity the difference, and that is the entire content of the word. It is an observed absence. It has no mechanism. Nothing reads it, because reading is by touch and there is nothing there to touch. Nothing moves it, stores it, holds it, follows it, or gives it back. No working has ever operated on it, in any recorded instance, by anyone, at any acuity. This is not a mystery awaiting a technique. It is the shape of the floor.

And the professionals use it anyway. A body two days dead will satisfy a short working before anybody standing near it will. Soldiers who have been in one bad fight work beside their own dead on purpose, and everyone understands why, and the men who arrange it deliberately are a different kind of person and are known to be.

What this costs, with nobody choosing cruelty:

A gallery crew shores a roof in a thin district, short of lime by a hand's weight. The set completes out of what is present. The man holding the lamp is sick for a month and the roof holds. Everyone did the job correctly.

Every layer-out has had to sit with a family on the third day and explain that the movement is contraction. They explain it well, because they have explained it many times. It does not help, and the family stays until the ninth day anyway.

A woman pulls her brother out of a collapse by solving four tons of brick with no pool under her and no flask left, and she does not stop when it runs out. He walks. She is hardened at both hands inside a year and reads nothing she did not already know. She is asked, often, whether she would do it again. She says yes, every time, and means it, and her hands are still what they are.

9The Law

Name your source,
or it'll name one for you.

Where it comes from. It is not an incantation. Nothing listens. It is a workface line — the sentence a foreman says at the top of a shift, in the same breath as check your footing and don't stand under the hoist — and it survives because it is the shortest true statement of failure mode four. Every trade that handles solving has its own drawl on it and none of them have improved it.

Who says it, and when. The caster says it, to herself, half-swallowed, immediately before a HEAVY or PREPARED working. Nobody says it before a FAST one; you would say it forty times in a fight and mean it none of them. Others say it at you: a sergeant to a green caster settling into cover, a foreman to a crew about to open a face, one professional to another as flat sarcasm when the other has just done something stupid and lived.

How an apprentice is taught it. Out loud, in full, pointing, for the first year. The master makes them speak the source before every stroke — the zinc in the flask, the copper in the wire, nothing else — and refuses the stroke if the sentence is vague. The speaking is a training wheel; the real naming happens in the read. It is drilled aloud so the habit is still there when the apprentice is frightened, because the read goes first when you are frightened.

Word What it is doing
Name An order, not advice. Naming is declaring the contents of the line you have already laid
your You own the boundary. Nobody lays it for you and nobody can be blamed for it after
source The body of matter the working takes substance from. Not the sap. The two are named separately and confusing them is the classic apprentice error
or Procedural, not moral. This is the next step, not a punishment
it'll The working. Deliberately unnamed. Everyone knows what it is and nobody enjoys saying so
name one for you The same verb. The working performs the identical act you declined to perform

The crack. The Law commands you to name your source. It does not say what a source may be. A caster who names a person and lays the boundary on them has followed it exactly, and the working proceeds correctly. Correct procedure and atrocity are the same act, performed with the same hands, in the same order.

The surrounding speech.

  • "Who's downstream?" — asked before cutting anything. Also asked, unkindly, of a person about to make a decision they have not thought through.
  • "Nothing goes short." — the shrug version of the Law. Said over a body when the arithmetic is obvious.
  • "He read it right and set it wrong." — an epitaph, and the most common one. Solve gets the respect; set gets the funerals.
  • "That's a nail's worth." — of trivial gain, or trivial harm. From the iron in a body. Deployed with a very level face.
  • "Set it or shift it." — shouted at half-set matter. Finish the working or move everyone out of the room; there is no third option and standing there discussing it is not one.

Naming key. Things here are named by the people who handle them, at speed, while tired. A real trade name is function plus material or place: tap house, cored stone, dazzle-plate, spent ground, cut face, drawing floor, the works. Real substances keep their real names — lime is lime, brass is brass — and renaming a material is the one unforgivable move, because the player must be able to do the arithmetic. Nothing is named after a person; things are named after the job. Formal names exist on paper and are never used aloud; the short name is the real name. Anything a soldier has to shout across a yard must be sayable in two seconds: two to four syllables, no apostrophes, no invented phonemes, no doubled vowels.

11Legibility — the Art-Direction Contract

An observer with no tooltip must be able to tell what happened and roughly what it cost. This section owns every visual and audible tell; nothing else in the document describes one.

The thermal grammar, and it is locked: drawing and solving run cold. Setting delivers heat. Half-set matter is body-temperature, always — the wrong thing sits at neither end, and that is how you know it is wrong.

  • SOLVE. Fingers flat on the material, a held half-second, then the shine dies and the surface goes matte. Fine dry haze lifts off it and stands there. The air over it goes cold. Silent.
  • SET. The haze collapses inward, edge to centre, with one hard snap and a breath of heat, and a sound like a heavy door seating.
  • SET GOING WRONG. A stutter of snaps instead of one. Stand back.
  • CHAINED STROKE. One gesture, no matte pause between lift and collapse. The hallmark of a professional and the thing to watch for.
  • BOUNDARY. The physical line — chalk, wire, cord, groove, or the rim of the caster's own hands — shows as a dry, dust-fine mark along its whole run the moment the stroke fires, across skin as readily as across stone. It is the single most-read thing on a battlefield: where does that line go, and am I inside it.
  • HALF-SET MATTER. A dull, matte, colourless mass with no highlights — light goes into it and does not come back. Haze over it neither rises nor falls. Edges shimmer very slightly, like heat off a road. Slumped, holding a shape it should not hold, body-temperature to the hand of anyone stupid enough. Almost no sound. It doesn't grow. It doesn't stop. It takes fingers off.
  • REACH. The dust-line jumps, in one frame, to a new object. Everything between goes untouched. That jump is the whole tell. A hard smell of wet stone, and the source shows it immediately — wood dropping half a finger's depth, ground going chalk-pale in a stripe, a man going grey and sitting down.
  • AIMED REACH. The same jump, but the line was already sitting wrong before the stroke — laid slack, laid past the material, laid behind the caster — and she was looking at a person, not at her work. Readable in advance by anyone who bothers to look, with a full two seconds to act.
  • ACCELERANT. Pouched fine and pale, and the only thing on a battlefield everyone else gives room to. Under draw the pouch frosts before the caster's hands do.
  • FIRE. The set's snap arrives as ignition instead of a breath of heat: one hard flat crack, and the boundary standing in flame along its whole run. The line burns — not the hand that drew it.
  • A RUNNER. The dust-line does not jump. It travels, at about the speed of a thrown stone, and the drag opens behind it — a scoured track with holes in it where it found something worth taking. A hand's width either side is untouched. It points back at whoever drew it.
  • RUNNING LOW. In order, and it clears with rest: hands white to the second knuckle; breath fogging in warm air; a fast fine tremor in the fingers; slurred consonants and a fixed stare.
  • OVERDRAWN — SLACK. A tremor that stays after the hands are still. A shortened stride on one side. A voice that runs out at the end of a sentence. It does not brush off and it does not come back.
  • OVERDRAWN — THINNING. Visible wasting, asymmetric, confined to what was under load. The limb is plainly smaller than its pair; skin over it is dry and takes a crease.
  • OVERDRAWN — HARDENING. Grey-white bloom at the nail beds, the corner of the mouth, the crease of an elbow. The part sounds wrong when tapped. Gait audible before it is visible.
  • OVERDRAWN — FAILURE. The part is dead weight and does not move. Obvious to a stranger at forty paces.
  • IMPLANT. Frost and heat at the seam; the seat showing through the skin under draw; grey skin two fingers' width around the join once it has taken an overdraw. This is why implanted casters wear sleeves.
  • RICH GROUND. Grass wrong-coloured and too tall in patches; standing water that won't clear; frost that doesn't take.
  • THIN GROUND. Lime blooming white on brick and plaster; hard water; crops failing from the field edges inward.
  • DEAD GROUND. Grey, level, dry and silent, with a hard rim where the drawing stopped.

12Progression

Progression is comprehension. Raw output is never the axis. A caster at the end of a campaign does not do bigger things. She does more things, faster, from worse material, in rooms other people think are empty.

Axis Verdict
Library depth The spine. Uncapped, purely earned, and it converts HEAVY workings into FAST ones. Every material read and retained is a permanent, visible widening of what a room offers
Stroke-chaining and technique Real, and the moment-to-moment one. Chaining is where mastery is felt in the hands. Trained, not bought
Sap infrastructure Real, at campaign scale, and political. A basin cut upstream changes every fight downstream of it. This is the axis that involves other people and gets people killed
Known ground Real and free, and it resets when you travel. Yesterday's corridor is today's advantage. Deliberately non-portable
Implants Real, and a trade, not a gift. They buy independence from pools and supply, they change what overdraw takes and what it takes it from, and they are a source an enemy can foul, block or cut. Power with a handle on the outside
Accelerant Not progression — supply. It lowers what a stroke costs, never what a caster is. Bought, carried, stolen, cut and used up. A campaign where advancement means better powder has gone wrong
Reagent quality and provenance Not progression — supply. It swings a fight hard and it can be stolen between fights. Never let a session's advancement be we bought better stock
Training acuity A trap, and the tutorial should say so out loud. It moves slowly, it has a hard personal ceiling, and a player who grinds it will feel cheated at hour forty. The low-acuity grinder wins on library, and the game must make that obvious early
Reading speed as its own stat A trap. There is no such stat. Recognising is faster than reading, so speed is library wearing a different coat

"Source."

"The floor."

"'The floor' isn't a source. It's a direction. What's in it?"

"...Stone."

"How much lime?"

"Enough."
