# Project Chimera — The Narrative Spine

*A story bible for a world where magic is transformation from a material source.*

Two things are required and they fail separately: the **material** in front of you, and the
**stock** under the floor. Everything in this document — the country, the man, the seven, the
machine, the woman hunting you and the thing you finally put down — comes out of that one
split.

## Part One - The Law

The old woman puts a flat hand on the floor of the shed before she does anything else, the way another woman would step out and look at the sky. It comes up cool against her palm, a draught out of the ground with no wind in it, and she grunts and gets off her knee. Good. There is something under the floor this morning.

Then the bench. A crock of madder root, ground and soaked since Tuesday. A pot of water at blood heat with a handful of lime in it, and four yards of grey cloth wrung out and waiting. She turns the cloth in with a stick and lays her hands on either side of the pot, and the girl watches her face and not her hands, because the face is where the work shows.

It comes on slow. The bath goes the colour of weak tea, and then of tea, and then of a new brick, and the shed smells of wet wool, and the old woman's arms are in to the elbow and her shoulders come down.

"That's a red," says the girl.

"That's the root," says the old woman. "The root was always going to be a red. All I did was find the heat, and the heat wasn't mine either."

She lifts the cloth out steaming and hangs it, and the floor under the two of them is colder than it was, and will be until spring.

### The Culture and Its Names

The Vessin hold a wet, cold, well-drained country of chalk downs, spring valleys and long river-crossings — a nation of dairies, dye-houses, breweries and bake-ovens, where the important buildings are the ones with chimneys. Their tongue is soft-mouthed and front-heavy. Stress falls on the first syllable always. The consonants are few: v, l, n, m, r, s, h, k, t, with the two liquid onsets *hl-* and *sv-*. There is no *th*, no *j*, no *w*, no hard *g*. Vowels double freely, and doubling means length, not force. Given names are two syllables and end in a vowel.

A family is known by what it brings to the common pot — the material it has always supplied to the village's cooking, curing and dyeing, held down the generations whether or not anyone still does the work. *Kesta*, madder root, gives Kestamer, the house that brings madder. *Sool*, salt, gives Soolmer. *Rann*, rennet, gives Rannmer; *aive*, wood-ash and lime, gives Aivemer; *vetta*, bone, for stock and for black dye, gives Vettamer; *lam*, tallow, gives Lammer. A woman who marries into a dye-house becomes a Kestamer, whatever she was born. A household that loses its material loses its name with it, and such people are called after the road they left on — *of the Kelaavi road*, said flat, with the house left out — which is an insult that outlives the people it was aimed at. To say a family brings nothing is the worst ordinary thing you can say in Vessin.

Twelve names, one tongue: Sevi Kestamer, Anka Soolmer, Hlemi Rannmer, Varo Aivemer, Neisa Vettamer, Osku Lammer, Talu Rannmer; and the places Kelaavi, Sootaavi, Hlemeska, Nateska and Varenuor.

### The Two Axes

The energy is called **stock**. It lies under the ground the way water lies under it: with a level, a season, a direction of seep, and a floor nobody has ever seen. It is rich under old wet valleys and thin under chalk. It seeps sideways, slowly. It can be tapped, held back by a stone floor, taken low by a hard summer, and fouled. It comes back at the speed of grass — which is to say over years. A parish that has been worked too hard is worked out, the same thing you say of a well. You feel for it with a flat palm on the floor: a cool draught rising means plenty, a dry stillness means the ground has nothing to give this side of spring. Under the floor it is stock; up in the work it is the fire. Same thing, two names, the way a sheep is a sheep in the field and mutton on the board.

The work is **setting** — the word a cook uses when a thing has taken and will not go back: a custard, a jelly, a dye. One who does it is a setter. Setters mend bone, close walls, turn poor grain into good, thicken milk and fix colour, and are given food and firewood for it. The trade is learned by feel, in sheds, from one old woman to one girl.

The two things a setting needs are the stock under you and the material in front of you, and they have nothing to do with each other. Sevi Kestamer has a full crock of madder root, a good bath, and cloth ready — everything a red is made of. But three dye-houses have worked Kelaavi all summer. She sets, and nothing rises; the bath stays the colour of weak tea, her hands go cold, and the little stock under her floor is gone into the attempt and will not be back before the turn of the year. The madder is untouched. In spring it will work perfectly.

The other way round is worse. Osku Lammer stands over the deep stock of Sootaavi with a pot of clean water, alone, in a shut room, and sets for wine. There is nothing in water that wine is made of, and no amount of stock and no amount of skill puts it there. What he gets is wine-coloured, smells of wine, sits in the cup like wine, and is water. Plenty under the floor does not put anything in the pot. It only makes the failure louder.

### The Law

**Nothing comes out of the pot that wasn't in the pot, and the fire's the ground's, not yours.**

It is said flat, twice through, to children learning to stir. The trades have worn it into their own shapes. Dyers: *no colour out of the bath that wasn't in the bath, and the heat's borrowed.* Bakers, knocking back dough: *you can't rise what isn't in the flour, and the oven isn't yours.* Masons: *the wall gives back the wall.* Midwives, who are the bluntest people in the country, say it over a hard birth: *nothing's in the child that wasn't in the mother, and the warmth comes up out of the floor.*

Every version says the same two things, and no version says a word about whose pot it is, or where the pot ends.

### The Three Failure Modes

**Flat.** The stock is short. The working stops before it does anything: the pot goes cold under your hands, nothing changes, and the stock you pulled is gone regardless. You have boiled an empty kettle. This is the lucky one. An apprentice sits through a hundred flat settings before she is allowed a full one, and does not believe it is the lucky one until she has seen the other two.

It is lucky in the room and not in the parish. A young setter at Hlemeska worked half a night on a carter with a chest wound over ground that had already done a dye-house summer, and she was right to try, and everyone in the yard said so at the time and said so afterwards. He died anyway. Eleven days later a child's arm went bad in the same village and there was nothing under any floor in it until the turn of the year, and the cart to Varenuor is four hours in good weather. Nobody in Hlemeska ever said a word to her about the carter. They simply stopped sending, and she was thirty before she understood why.

**Hollow.** The stock is enough, the material is short, and nothing near enough holds what is missing. The working finishes anyway, out of what is actually present, and it keeps the outside exactly right. The rule never varies: *the shape is held, and the gap is filled with whatever the pot has most of.* In flesh that is water, then fat. In grain it is water, then chaff. In stone and soil it is water, then clay, then ash. Name the commonest thing in the pot and picture the missing part made of it, and you will be right.

A setter mends a smith's cracked forearm, taking from the smith himself, who has been hungry since autumn and has not the mineral in him. The bone knits straight and clean and the right length. It is tallow and water. It is white when it is cut, it warms to blood heat, and it bends over the following year. He can hold a cup. He will never hold a hammer again. A village sets bread from chaff and a bucket off the well: loaf, crust, crumb, smell and taste all correct, and the substance was never in the chaff, so the bread is water and air. It fills a stomach, satisfies hunger, and feeds nobody. Hlemeska lost forty people one winter with good bread on the table. A mason short of lime closes a wall; the joints take and look like mortar for a season, then dry to a grey dust a thumb can rub out — and because every joint is the same age, the wall does not fail in one place, it fails in all of them at once, in the first hard frost.

Hollow work is not cursed and does not revert. It was honestly made. It simply sits there being wrong for fifty years, and — this is the part that kills careful people — it reads as sound. A hollow loaf has the smell of bread and a hollow patch has the grain of stone. Set from mended material and your hands tell you a full pot that is water and dust. The poorest villages mend everything twice, which makes them the most dangerous ground in the country to work on, and why a careful setter asks a stranger's wall who closed it before she puts a hand on it.

**Reaching.** The stock is enough, the material is short, and something in the pot does hold what is wanted. The working goes and gets it. It is not clever and it does not choose. It takes the loosest thing first and takes it empty before it moves on, and looseness is the only thing it knows.

The order everyone teaches is a rule of thumb about what is usually loosest in a room, and it is a good one: whatever is already in or touching the pot; then loose stores standing near, the flour in the sack, the salt in the crock, the water in the pail; then growing things; then animals; then people. But the order underneath it is plain looseness, and looseness is not the same as being alive or dead. A supper an hour old in a man's stomach is a looser thing than a standing crop, which is held into its own growing, or a live sheep, which is held into being a sheep. Feed a parish well and you have not made it safe. You have made it the first thing a short pot goes to.

Within any body it keeps the same habit. The warmth off the skin first, because heat is the loosest thing there is; then the last meal, then fat, then water and blood, then the teeth, then muscle, then bone. A well-fed man near a small shortfall loses the heat off his hands, his dinner, and a night's sleep. A thin one has no dinner and no fat to give, so it goes straight to his water and his blood, and after that to his teeth. It takes the same off everybody, which is why you can tell who is poor by their mouths.

In the trades they say the pot takes. It is the working that takes and the pot that gives, but nobody talks that way with their hands in the water.

You can feel which of the three you are getting. A working that comes on smooth and slow is sound. One that goes dull and cold under the hands is going flat. One that rises *eagerly* — warm, fast, better than you hoped — is short of material and coming out hollow; good feel is bad news. And when it reaches, the cold arrives from the wrong direction: not up out of the floor but sideways, out of the room. Setters call that **the turn**, and the whole of the answer is to get your hands off.

Your hands lie to you in two places, and both are taught early. They read the room and not the pot, so a living body standing near — a neighbour with the lamp, a child in the doorway — makes them come up full when the pot is empty. And a small shortfall, less than a handful of stuff, reaches with no turn at all: it feels like clean work. That is why an honest setter of thirty years finds at the end that the mice never lived in her shed, that her dog aged strangely, that her girl bruises like an old woman. There was nothing to feel. There was nothing to see.

### Standing Near

A thing is in the pot if it shares water with the pot. That is the whole rule. Steam is water going where the room takes it, which is why the old test is a good one and why setters use it: they watch where the steam goes before they begin, and a bone-setting makes no steam of its own, so a careful woman carries a bowl of hot water in with her and stands it on the floor to see. Touching is surest. A wet floor, a shared trough, a millrace, a wet hem, a dog drinking out of the same pail — all of that is touching, at any distance. Dry is out, and dry is out absolutely: a man standing three feet off on dry flags is further out of that pot than a man a mile down the valley with his boots in the leat.

Inside the pot, warm and wet things go first and cold dry things go last, which is a matter of looseness and not of safety. Stone and cold draughts and an open door make a room less one thing. A shut warm kitchen with a pot on the floor is one pot from wall to wall — and the season you most need mending, when there is frost and broken legs and bread that will not rise, is exactly the season the door is shut and the fire is up and the whole family is inside it. Mending is a safe trade in August.

So: work alone, door shut, floor dry, dog outside, and nothing standing in water. Children in Vessin are taught to step back onto dry ground when they see someone kneel to a working, the way children elsewhere are taught about ice. A setting can be safe for an hour and then take a woman's hand off, because somebody came in with wet boots.

### The Loophole

The Law says nothing comes out of the pot that wasn't in it, and it has never said where the pot ends. Anything sharing water with the working is in it, and a person is made of exactly what a loaf or a bone or a wall runs short of. You cannot use yourself, because the fire comes up out of the ground through your feet and your legs and out at your palms, and nothing goes back down a chimney. So when the pot is short you put somebody else's hand in the water on purpose, before the working goes hunting the room on its own. You feed her first so it takes her dinner and not her blood, you work slow so it comes off the top of her, and that is the whole of it.

The one who sits is called **a hand**. Bread and butter and salt, all she will take, before she sits; her hand in the water to the wrist; still. Then you feed her again after, and walk her home, and if she has given once this month you send her away and go short instead. She goes home cold and shaking and ravenous, sleeps twelve hours, eats double for two days and is right by the end of the week. That is all. It does not read as murder because it is not murder; it is closer to giving somebody a lift on a hard day. It is also the only thing that keeps the working off the child in the doorway, which is why a decent person agrees and a decent person asks.

There are courtesies, and they are the trade's whole conscience. Nobody eats before the hand. The house gives meat afterwards if it has meat. You do not ask a hungry man, because a hungry man has nothing loose in him and the pot will go to his blood. You do not ask a woman carrying, for the same reason and worse: she has nothing loose either, and what she has instead is being used. That is the midwives' half of the Law with the teeth showing. In a hard birth the mother is the pot and the material both, and she cannot be her own hand any more than the setter can, so the sister or the neighbour sits, and if nobody sits and the child is short of mineral, it comes out the right shape and the right weight, and lives about two years.

The setter cannot sit either, ever, for anyone, and that has a consequence nobody thinks about until it is thirty years old. A setter with a broken arm must be mended by another setter, generally in a poor parish, generally from a pot that is short. Mended material reads full. So every setter in Vessin who was ever a patient carries a little water and tallow that her own hands cannot see past, and the best setters are the oldest, and the oldest have all been mended.

One hand at one hearth is a shared meal and a favour returned. Now do it at every crossing, oven and dye-house in the country, in turns, on a bench with a trough, all day, for two hundred years. The courtesies are the first thing to go, because they are courtesies and not the work. The bread comes before the hand sits, then it comes after, then it is a coin instead. The ones who come back most often are the ones who need the loaf, and they are exactly the ones with nothing loose left to give. Nobody starves. Everybody is a meal behind, always, and a little cold, and the children come up shorter than their grandparents. There is no book. Nobody ever thought to start one, because a book is for things that were done to people.

### Resurrection

A dead body holds nearly everything a person is made of, in the right amounts, already assembled, and is short of exactly one thing: the quiet work that living flesh does all day and all night without being asked. That is not a substance sitting anywhere. It cannot be supplied, brought, or stood near. So the pot is always short, and there is always something in the room that is still working, and the setting therefore never comes out flat and never comes out hollow. It reaches, every time, for the same thing, and finds it in the people standing there.

What stands up is called **a risen**. The body warms, breathes, opens its eyes and sits up inside a quarter of an hour. It is not the dead person and never was.

Reaching for material goes by the fixed order, meal and fat and water and teeth. This is not that. What a risen is short of is the working of living, so what it takes is the working of living, in its own order, off everyone inside the steam: their warmth first, then their last few days, then the newest things in their heads, then the trade in their hands — and a trade goes off in two parts, the knowing before the doing, because the knowing sits above it. So a risen moves like the sister who was holding the hand, uses the mother's turns of phrase, knows the shape of a stranger's kitchen, and cannot say its own name. It goes on taking for as long as living things stay near it. Nothing is given back. The sister does not remember her wedding. The midwife's hands are ordinary now; she can still splint, and she can no longer tell you which of two breaks to splint first.

The practice is old and unheroic and every parish knows it. Two gravediggers are fetched, never relatives. They put a rope on it and walk it out at a rope's length, changing places at the near end so the taking is shared between them, out to bare ground or **white ground** with nothing living inside the steam of it, and then they stand well off. It goes quiet in an afternoon and cold by evening, and they bury it where it stopped, and the parish does not say the person's name over it. Nobody speaks to it on the way. It answers with what it has taken, and answering teaches it where to take more. *Don't answer it* is the whole of the instruction, and it is very hard to follow, because it is using your mother's voice. Every district has one old woman who has done this four or five times and will come if you send for her.

### Pulling Too Hard

Pull harder than the ground under you will give and the difference comes out of the setter, in this order, and nothing reverses. The ruin takes what a body can best spare and takes it in turn, which means a man with nothing left to spare at one stage does not stop there; he goes down two.

**First the taste goes**: salt and nothing else, forever. In a country of dairies and dye-houses and bake-ovens the tongue is the instrument, and a setter who has lost it can no longer tell a bath is ready, or a mash, or milk from milk on the turn. He does not merely salt his food twice and eat it sadly. He has stopped being any use at the other half of his living, and is now dependent on the thing that is eating him. A paled mother cannot taste that the milk is off, and feeds it to her house.

**Then the colour goes**: hair, eyes and skin fade to the shade of unbleached cloth, no dye will hold on him, and no bruise will show. That last is not a curiosity. The country's plainest way of telling that somebody has been sitting at the bench too often is the look of her arms, and a woman far enough along has arms that cannot report anything ever again.

**Then he goes cold**: sits by the fire in August, cold to hold, cuts open for weeks, and his wife knows before he does. A cold man is also very hard to mend, because he is the tightest thing in any room and a short pot goes straight past him into whoever is holding his hand.

**Last, the skin thickens** and the joints set a little more each year until he is bedbound and entirely sensible and cannot turn his head.

The land goes the same way and can be read from a cart. **First the dew stops** — grass still green, mornings dry, and the farmers argue about the weather for a year. **Then the wells go flat**: the water runs clear and tastes of nothing, bread will not rise on it and dye will not take in it, which is how a dye-house village finds out first. **Then the ground goes the colour of dry straw**, grass and leaves and the coats of animals all fading while still alive; and it does not spread in a ring around the working, it goes downhill and along the seep, in a long tongue, sometimes a mile from where anybody stood, which is why the farmers argue about the weather and are not stupid to. Sheep off that ground grow wool no colour will hold. **Last is white ground**: not burnt, just finished. Nothing grows on it and nothing rots on it — a dead ewe dries out whole.

There is nothing under white ground, so nothing can be set on it. There is also less and less under the ground around it, because a level slopes towards a drained place the way water does, and white ground therefore widens by drinking its neighbours long after anybody has worked there. That is the whole of its safety and the whole of its menace: it is the one place in Vessin where no working can touch you, and it is the one place that gets bigger on its own. People build on it.

### The Vocabulary

1. **stock** — the life energy under the ground; has a level, seeps, can be tapped and taken low, and comes back at the speed of grass. In a working it is called the fire, which is the same thing in use.
2. **setting** — the work itself, and to do it; one who does it is a setter, which already means bonesetter in ordinary speech, and that is most of what they do.
3. **the pot** — everything a working counts as its source: the material in front of you and everything sharing water with it.
4. **flat** — the clean failure, stock short, nothing changed and the stock gone regardless.
5. **hollow** — completion with the shape correct and the substance wrong, the gap filled with whatever the pot had most of.
6. **reaching** — completion by taking the shortfall out of whatever is nearest and loosest, which has no opinion about what it takes it from.
7. **the turn** — the moment a working starts to reach, felt as cold arriving sideways out of the room instead of up out of the floor.
8. **a hand** — a person who puts a hand in the water so the working takes from them on purpose instead of hunting the room.
9. **a risen** — what stands up when someone sets on the dead: warm, breathing, made of slices off the living, and never the person.
10. **white ground** — land taken past coming back; not blighted or cursed, only used up, pale and quiet, and therefore the safest ground there is.

## Part Two - The Man Who Cut Something Out

#### Candidate 1: Halu Aivemer — the man who made the taking visible

**What he cut out.** His warmth. Not his temper: the heat his body makes. A risen takes the warmth off a room in the first minute, before it has been through anybody's dinner, which tells any setter plainly that heat sits above the food and is the loosest thing in a person there is — and a loose thing can be taken like fat or water. So Halu sat as a hand. Vensa Kestamer worked a dye bath gone cold in a cold shed at Hlemeska, over sound ground, a pot short of nothing at all but heat, and Halu's arms were in it to the elbow. It took the loose heat off him in the first minute, which is ordinary and happens to hands every day. He had her keep going. It went on down into the making of the heat, and after some hours there was nothing left in him that produces it. He needed her, and he needed her to break the one courtesy that matters — she had to keep working a hand who was cold and shaking and past giving, and she had to do it for four hours by the light of a lamp somebody else held in the doorway.

**Why almost nobody does it.** Because you must lie still in cold water and ask a decent person to go on hurting you, hour after hour, with nothing to feel but yourself going out; and because the thing you are getting is nothing. You gain no new ability. Nobody is warmed by it, nobody is fed by it, and there is no moment where it becomes impressive. A coward stops at the second hour because it is unbearable. A clever man stops at the second hour because he has worked out that he is giving up a great deal for the privilege of never again being any use to anybody at a bench.

**What he became.** Halu is in every pot he stands in and is the last thing in all of them. He has no loose heat, no fat worth the name and no appetite, so a short working with his hands in the water goes past him to the dog, the flour, the girl by the door, every time. He can therefore be present at every working in the country and never be the one it comes to — which is exactly how he came to be present at every working in the country. What he lost with it: he can never sit as a hand, and he cannot feel the turn, because the turn is cold arriving sideways and he has been cold from the inside for thirty-two years. Across a table he looks like a hard-pulled setter of sixty: pale, dry, cold to hold. Cuts on him stay open for weeks. He keeps hot bricks in his chair and his rooms at proving heat, and he tells people it is his joints.

**What he still wants, which proves the removal failed.** Bread and butter and salt, all he will take, standing, before every working: the meal you feed the one who is about to give, so the pot takes the newest thing off the top of her. Halu cannot give and has not given since he was thirty-eight. He eats it anyway, standing, and is short with anybody who eats before he does.

**His goal, stated so a reader agrees with it.** Nobody starves in Vessin. That is the sentence everyone is proud of, and it is the sentence that hides the country. Go and stand at any crossing, at any oven, at any dye-house, and watch who sits at the bench. It is the same six people every week; they are the six who need the loaf; they are the six with nothing loose in them; and the pot goes to their blood because it has nowhere better to go. Nobody did that. Every one of them sat down to it. Every one of them was thanked. The grandchildren are two inches shorter than the grandparents, everyone is a meal behind and a little cold, and not one person can be named who did it. That is the whole trouble. Harm spread thin enough is invisible, and invisible harm never stops, because nothing stops what nobody can see.

So gather it up. Do a district's whole season of work in a few great workings on deep ground, in daylight, with the parish standing where it can watch, and take what it takes from people who came for that purpose and are fed for a week afterwards. One district feeds forty in one afternoon, in the open, in front of the whole parish, and everybody can see the size of it. A visible harm can be argued with. It can be refused. It can be argued down to something smaller. It can be passed round. Halu is not asking anyone to suffer more. He is asking them to suffer where it is the size of a thing you can see.

He has a rule, and he says it the same way every time: no house sits twice in a season; the parish that gives is fed for a week out of the parish that did not; and the taking is said out loud before it begins, in front of everybody, and said again after, so that a man who wants to argue knows the size of what he is arguing with. In one district he has already done it. The bench there is firewood. The coin is gone. A woman who used to sit eleven times a year sits once, in August, on ground that can carry it, and eats meat for a week, and her children are taller than the next parish's, and she thinks well of him. He built none of the machine and says so. He is the first man in eight generations to see the whole of it and know what it is for, and the first to set a date.

The date is for the proof. His argument is that shortfall is a question of size and nothing else, and that a shortfall divided finely enough stops being an injury and becomes a spoonful. Everyone says the dead cannot be brought back because the pot is always short. Everyone says it having never once seen a pot with a country in it. So: at a crossing, in daylight, on a woman the parish knew and buried, with the spoonful said out loud beforehand — and if a country gives a spoonful each and Vensa Kestamer walks, then the benches come out of every village in Vessin and nobody's daughter ever sits again. *I chose her because a parish will take a proof from a woman they knew.* He is entirely sincere, he is well within the rules as anyone in the country understands them, and he is wrong in one place only.

**His decline.** He was a hand at nineteen. He cut out his warmth at thirty-eight and has not given since; he is seventy. Because he had already given up the cold stage before he ever pulled hard, the ordinary ruin came for him and found a rung missing, and he went down two. The taste went first, as it does; he eats alone now and salts twice, and he asks other people whether the bread is good and cannot tell whether they are being kind. The colour went over four years, and no dye will hold on him, which for an Aivemer is a joke people stopped making. Then the thickening came on him twenty years early. His knuckles have set. His neck turns less each spring. He has not risen from a chair unassisted since he was forty-eight; he is carried in and seated before the doors open, and his coat is cut to hide that his elbows no longer straighten. Downhill and west-by-north of his three great sites the ground has gone the colour of dry straw in a long tongue that follows the seep and not the working, and he says it is the weather, and for now the farmers agree with him.

**The counter-argument the story will make against him.** He cannot feel the turn. He has built a country-sized working out of daylight and volunteers and a bowl of bread, and the one instrument that would tell him when it stops taking from the people who came for that purpose and starts taking from the crowd is the instrument he gave up in order to be allowed near it. This is never said aloud. It is shown by a room filling up. The people he gathered to witness the taking are, by standing there in the warm, in the steam, on wet flags, in the pot — and the working does not care that they came to watch. The argument against Halu is made by a hundred honest people doing exactly what he told them to do, correctly, and by his own hands finding a full pot at the moment the room goes cold from the wrong direction and he alone cannot tell.

#### Candidate 2: Marno Rannmer — the man drinking the country dry to save it

**What he cut out.** What his hands knew. Not the craft: the sense underneath it that reads a room and tells you flat, hollow, or the turn. Nothing can take a thing off a setter, because a setter is the flue and material travels into a pot and never out of one. There is exactly one arrangement in the world that gets round that, and it is a risen, because the risen is the pot and you are the room. Marno set on a stranger's body in a bare house with nothing else living inside the steam of it, and stood in reach while it worked down the order: his warmth, then his last few days, then whatever happened to be newest in his head, then his trade — and a trade goes off knowing first and doing after. He got two gravediggers to come, not to walk the risen out but to walk him out, on a rope, and they counted aloud and pulled him off at four hundred, in the middle of the last part, so the doing stayed in his hands and the knowing under it went.

**Why almost nobody does it.** You cannot choose what is newest in your head, you cannot feel from inside where you are in the order, and nobody has come back up from below the hands. So the whole of it rests on two men, counting correctly, in a room where the only other person who could correct them is the thing on the floor using your mother's voice.

**What he became.** He cannot be warned. He works by cart-reading, by asking who closed a wall before he touches it, and by a boy with a lamp standing outside on dry ground who calls in when the room goes cold. He is the calmest man in any shed, because nothing in his hands ever objects. He also cannot tell sound work from hollow, and never will again, and everything he has closed in eleven years he has closed blind. Across a table he is a warm, ordinary, rather kind man of fifty who is missing eleven days from the year he was thirty and has stopped mentioning it.

**What he still wants, which proves the removal failed.** Coming into any room, he puts a flat palm on the floor. Inns, kitchens, other people's houses, the back step of a church. He does it before he takes his coat off, he gets nothing, and he does it again in the next room.

**His goal, stated so a reader agrees with it.** There is nothing under white ground, so nothing can be set on it, so it is the safest land in this country; everyone knows it and everyone builds on it. Say that out loud twice and then explain to a child why we do not all live there. The wells come back at the speed of grass and we have been drinking since before anyone's great-grandmother. Every parish pulls harder each year on less; every year the poorest sit at the bench more often, because a thinner pot needs more of them to finish; and the country's answer is to be careful, which is what the country has been for two hundred years while getting steadily shorter and colder. Careful is not a plan. Careful is how you go down slowly. There is exactly one condition under which nobody's daughter is ever a hand again, and it is not virtue and it is not the Law: it is empty ground. Marno intends to finish the wells. He will pull the parishes flat, one district at a time, starting with the ones already worked out — and the ground around a finished parish thins on its own afterwards, sloping in the way water slopes, which means the work half-does itself once begun. He means to hand the grandchildren a pale, quiet, safe country where the worst thing that can happen in a kitchen is that the bread does not rise.

**His decline.** He salts twice and has for six years. His hair and eyes have gone the shade of unbleached cloth, and he wears a hat indoors and says it is the light. The cold came last winter and his wife knew before he did. He pulls harder every season because the ground he has left is thinner, which is the shape of the whole project — the more of it he finishes, the less able he is to finish the rest — and the straw colour now moves at a walking pace along the valleys behind him where it used to move a foot a year.

**The counter-argument the story will make against him.** Marno cannot tell hollow from sound, and he has spent eleven years closing walls, mending arms and setting bread in the villages he is emptying, because he is not a monster and he mends what he passes. The story will show a frost. Every joint he closed is the same age, so the walls do not fail in one place; they fail in all of them at once, and the people under them are the people he was going to save by finishing the wells six years from now. Nobody kills Marno with an argument about the future. The future arrives in February.

#### Candidate 3: Sero Vettamer — the man who made himself the proof

**What he cut out.** His substance. Over nine years he sat as a hand far too often and on purpose — not fed first, not worked slow — until the last meal, the fat, and a great deal of the water and blood had gone out of him; and then he had a setter close him back up out of a pot he knew was short. The room was stripped bare and the door was shut and both of them knew why. The shape was held and the gap was filled with what the pot had most of. Sero is, from the shoulders down, largely tallow and water. He needed a setter willing to mend from a pot she knew was short, which is the one thing every good setter is trained never to do; she did it four times and then hanged herself, and he says her name at table.

**Why almost nobody does it.** Because hollow work does not revert and cannot be undone, there is no stage at which you feel wrong, and the whole of it arrives years later all at once. You are not brave for a night. You are comfortable for a decade and then you cannot lift what you lifted last year, and your forearms bend, and your teeth go, and on every one of those days you had the option of stopping and it never once felt urgent.

**What he became.** Mended material reads full. Sero walks into a shed and every setter's hands come up full, because hands read the room and not the pot, and he is a room-sized lie. Workings near him come on eagerly — warm, fast, better than anyone hoped — and finish hollow, and nobody's hands warn them. He can therefore prove hollow to any village in an afternoon without touching anything. Across a table he is a heavy, healthy-looking man with a good colour who does not pick things up. He carries a knife and opens his own forearm to show that it is white when it is cut.

**What he still wants, which proves the removal failed.** He keeps a bonesetter's splint-roll in his coat, and he cannot set on flesh any more without the work coming out hollow, and he knows it. He re-rolls it every night before he sleeps.

**His goal, stated so a reader agrees with it.** Reaching gets the songs, and reaching at least is honest — it takes from somebody and that somebody knows. Hollow is what actually kills, and it kills at a distance and in silence and with the table looking well laid. Hlemeska lost forty people with good bread in front of them. The poorest villages mend everything twice, so their walls and their bread and their bones are already water and chaff, and the next setting taken off that material is worse, and the one after that is worse again, and every step of it was somebody being kind. So stop. No mending in a parish that has been mended. Let the wall fall while it is a wall and not a winter. Let the arm stay broken. It is a hard thing to say to a man with a broken arm, and Sero says it to his face, and he is right, and everyone who hears him knows he is right, which is why they hate him.

**His decline.** He is not a heavy puller, so the ordinary stages came late: taste last spring, colour not yet. What is finishing him is his own mending. His forearms have bent a finger's width in two years, three teeth are gone, and the thickening is now setting joints that are made of tallow, which does not go stiff so much as it stops coming back. He hides it by never lifting anything in company and by keeping a boy to carry his bag, and he has taken to doing his demonstrations sitting down.

**The counter-argument the story will make against him.** He is right about hollow and wrong about people, and the story proves it by letting him win. A parish takes his advice and stops mending. The wall stands unmortared, the arm stays crooked, the bread is honest and there is not enough of it, and by March they are doing what the poor have always done and mending twice at night without telling him, because a man with a broken arm cannot wait nine years to be proved right. Sero could not follow his own rule either. He had himself mended four times.

### The Pick

**Halu Aivemer.** He is the only one whose project becomes ordinary furniture — a bench, a trough, a bowl of bread and butter and salt, and a line of people who came of their own accord and were thanked. Marno needs villages emptied and Sero needs villages to refuse him. Halu needs nothing but decent behaviour, performed correctly, in public, forever, and that is the machine the rest of this bible is describing. The grief comes from people getting it right.

From Marno, graft the ruin you can read from a cart: the straw colour running downhill and along the seep from Halu's three great sites, argued about as weather by farmers who are not wrong to argue. From Sero, graft the hollow proof: Halu's most admired workings — the long crossing at Varenuor, the winter bread of two districts — are hollow, honestly made, reading as sound, and he is the one man present who cannot feel it.

### The Man Himself

He talks like a lime-burner explaining a kiln to a nephew: short sentences, ordinary words, one thing at a time, and he waits after each one to see whether you have it. He does not raise his voice and does not gloat, and when he is contradicted he asks a question about your own trade — how you know a bath is ready, how you know when to knock a dough back — and then uses your answer for the next hour as though you had given him a gift. People come out of rooms with him having agreed to things, and cannot afterwards find the place where they were pushed.

Being with him is warm and wrong. His rooms are kept at proving heat, always, and there is a smell of hot brick. He is seated when you come in and seated when you leave. He will not shake hands twice; the first time is enough to tell you he is cold, and he has learned that the second time is the thing people remember.

Trade-hands: an Aivemer's hands, wood-ash and lime, cracked across the backs and cracked open at the knuckles, and the cracks stay open for weeks because of what he is. He keeps them in his sleeves and out of the light, and he never once puts a flat palm to the floor, which sets him apart from every other setter alive.

The habit that gives him away is the meal. Bread and butter and salt, all he will take, standing, before every working — the meal for the one who is about to give, eaten by the one man in the country who cannot — and nobody in the room eats before he does.

He was a lime-burner's son in Sootaavi with a mother who sat at the bench for whoever asked, and then he was doing it himself, at nineteen, at Varenuor, twice in one month because the house needed the loaf. He remembers the setter apologising. He remembers the woman on the bench beside him who came three times and died in the spring of nothing anybody could name, and that nobody ever said she had died of anything. He will say all of that, once, quietly, to the face of whichever protagonist has come to stop him, and she will have no answer, and he will not press it, because he does not need to. He has one further sentence for her and he says it early, so the story has to live with it: *take my working away and you will have hands at more hearths than you had before, and it will be the same six people at every one of them.*

The scene the story will eventually show is the shed at Hlemeska: a cold room, good ground, a dye bath gone off heat, his arms in it to the elbow, and Vensa Kestamer feeding him bread and butter and salt first, because she was decent. Four hours. Him saying *go on* at the end of each of them, in that same flat voice, and her doing it, and the boy in the doorway holding the lamp and standing, as he had been taught since he could walk, well back on dry ground.

## Part Three - The Machine That Is a Country

### The machine is the water

Vessin is chalk. Rain falls on the downs and disappears into them, and comes up again ten miles off in the spring valleys, cold and clean and never where you want it. Everything the country does with its hands wants water in quantity: dairies wash, dye-houses bathe, breweries mash, tanneries steep, bake-ovens want a wet floor and a good well at the door. So the Vessin have spent their whole history taking hold of their water. They cap spring-heads and lead them in stone. They cut leats to the dairies and races to the mills. They floor the drains that keep the wet hollows from standing all winter. They lay elm pipe from a good spring to a village with a bad one, and at every crossing, where the road wants a bridge and the bridge wants the river held in one channel, they build the same three things they have always built: an arch, a leat, and a long stone trough.

This is real work and it is done well. The drains are why Sootaavi is pasture instead of marsh and why the dairies there have not had a bad summer in a century. The capped springs are why nobody in Vessin has drunk fever water in living memory. The troughs water beasts, fill buckets, take the overflow in a wet March and hold water back against a dry August. A Vessin child grows up believing, correctly, that the water-work is the good thing their country does. When the crossing at Varenuor was rebuilt, three parishes came out to watch the centring struck, and stood in the rain to do it.

Two things about that work are true and are never said in the same breath.

The first is that stone floors dam stock. Every setter knows it in the small: you lay a course of slabs under a dye-vat to keep your bath off your neighbour's ground, four courses laid the short way, and a good dyer will tell you it is worth the labour twice over. The drains of Vessin are stone-floored for four hundred miles, and the courses are laid across the fall of the land rather than with it, and what they hold back is not water.

The second is that anything sharing water with the pot counts as touching. A millrace puts things in the pot that are nowhere near it. The leats join the races, the races join the drains, the drains take the pipe, the pipe fills the trough, and the trough is where the hand sits with her wrist in the water while the bread is set. There is now no crossing, no oven and no dye-house in Vessin whose water does not run, by some path, into the water of every other. The plumbing of the nation is one pot from the downs to the sea.

### Three people doing their jobs correctly

**Talu Rannmer** keeps the leat at Hlemeska, as her mother did. She walks it twice a week with a rake and a hook, pulls the weed, lifts the silt, chocks the sluice, and swears at boys who dam it for fun. She knows her mile the way a cook knows a pan. She knows the stretch below the elder runs warm and weeds up first, and that the two hundred yards past the chapel must never be let go dry, because that is the rule her mother gave her and it is a good rule — a dry leat cracks its bed, and a cracked bed is a fortnight's work and a summer of bad water. She has never seen the parish from above. She has never met anyone who keeps a different mile. She has never had cause to wonder why a rule about cracked beds was handed down with the weight of a rule about wells.

She is the person in this section you would like at once, and nothing she does is wrong.

**Varo Aivemer** is a crossing-mason. He rebuilds footings, relays trough-beds, points arches, and works to the pattern his trade has always worked to: below the waterline, dry-jointed, courses laid the short way, no lime. He knows this is peculiar and he has said so at table. Dry joints below water are more labour and less strength, and lime is the very thing his own house brings; he could put his hand on the crock. His master's answer was the trade's answer — *lime moves in that water, it always has* — and it has the ring of the kind of true thing a trade knows without ever having proved it. What he complains about is the relaying. A dry-jointed footing comes up under a bar as easy as a floor slab; he can lift a course single-handed with a crow and a plank, and he says so with feeling, because a course that lifts that easy is a course that shifts in a flood and has to be lifted again every nine or ten years, and he has done it nine times in thirty. He can tell you the pattern is old. He cannot tell you that the same pattern, laid at the same depth, is under every crossing on that river from the chalk to the sea, because he has worked on four of them and no man works on all of them. Everything that makes Vessin a good place to live was built by men like him, correctly, for reasons they could give you.

**Anka Soolmer** walks a river. That is all it is called and it is a great deal of work. She goes the length of it twice a year, up one bank and down the other, palm on the floor of every dairy and dye-house and bake-house she passes, and she tells the parishes which ground is worked out and which is coming back, and which dye-houses ought to wait for spring, and where the next drain wants cutting. She reads the country the way a farmer reads weather, and she is right often enough that people do what she says. She has stopped three villages from working themselves pale and is prouder of that than of anything else in her life, and she is right to be. Twice at Kelaavi she stood in the doorway of a dye-house and would not move, and the dyers hated her for a year, and Kelaavi still has grass. She has noticed that the resting always seems to come out favouring a seep that runs west-by-north, and she has assumed, reasonably, that this is because the chalk lies that way. She has never seen the country entire. Nobody alive has. The shape of the thing is only visible at the size of a nation across two hundred years, and a working life is neither.

None of the three is a conspirator. All three would be insulted by the suggestion. The apparatus does not need conspirators; it needs competent people who do a good job and hand the job on, which is exactly what Vessin is best at.

### Why it takes generations

Because stock comes back at the speed of grass, and there is no way to hurry grass. To raise the level under one place you must dam the seep with stone and then leave the ground alone while it fills, and filling a parish takes decades, and filling a valley takes lifetimes. Every drain-floor laid is a dam that will not show its work until the men who laid it are dead. Meanwhile the country still has to eat, so the working has to be moved elsewhere while the ground rests — which is what the resting seasons are. Genuine husbandry, honestly meant, well argued about at every crossing, and also a slow steering of the whole nation's stock down toward one low ground.

The material axis cannot be hurried either, and it cannot be stored. Material does not keep. In the hungry decade a village above Sootaavi salted down a barrel against the winter and set from it in February, out of the barrel, and got a hollow loaf — because salt beef in a barrel is salt and water and gristle and none of the rest of what a loaf is short of, and a barrel standing in a cold outhouse gives up nothing anyway, cold holding back the way it does. They ate the barrel like men, in the ordinary way, with a knife. Nobody has tried it since, and the trades say it flat: you cannot lay down a crock of a person against a hard year. The only way to have a great deal of material standing near a working is to have a great many living, well-fed people joined to it by water, and people are grown, not gathered.

Worse, mended material is no good. A village that mends everything twice is water and dust all through, and a pot full of hollow people is a pot full of nothing. So the apparatus needed the country to become prosperous, well-drained, well-watered and honestly fed, and needed that to be true for a hundred years running, before it was worth doing at all.

And here is the part that turns kindness into machinery. A working takes the loosest thing first. In a room, loose and near are the same thing, so the order is taught the way it is taught — what is touching the pot, then the loose stores standing about, then growing things, then animals, then people — and a girl learning in a shed will never once find that order wrong, because in a shed it never is. At the size of a country the two come apart. A sheep in a field is alive and whole and holds nothing loose; a man an hour after his dinner is holding a meal on top of himself that is barely his yet. Spread the pot from the downs to the sea and the hedges and the herds are not first any more. The loosest thing in Vessin is whoever has most recently eaten well.

Which is why the good half of the apparatus is not a disguise for the bad half. It is the bad half. Feed the country honestly and the country is loose. Sootaavi's dairies, the clean springs, the bread on the tables of eleven parishes: that is the mash coming up to heat.

### What it is for, and how near it is

One working, on one body, in one room.

A setting on the dead always reaches, because the thing a corpse is short of is not a substance and cannot be brought or stood near. The apparatus does not solve that. It does something that looks from a distance like solving it: it spreads the reaching so thin that nobody anywhere feels the turn. A shortfall of less than a handful takes with no turn at all — it comes on smooth, it feels like clean work, there is nothing to feel and nothing to see. If the pot is the whole of Vessin, then every living soul in the country gives about a spoonful and a night's sleep, and nothing is felt, and nothing is seen, and what stands up will have been made out of a nation instead of out of a kitchen.

It will still be a risen. It will still not be the person. It will go on taking off every living thing that stays near it for as long as it stands, and by then everything living in Vessin is near it, joined wrist and trough and race and drain, and there is nobody standing far enough off to put a rope on it.

Halu does not believe this. He believes the failure was always one of quantity — that the old settings on the dead came out wrong because a farmhouse kitchen has four people in it and a rope and two frightened gravediggers, and that a thing done badly a thousand times is not evidence about the thing, only about the kitchens. He is making, at the size of a country, the mistake a man makes when he stands over deep ground with a pot of clean water and sets for wine. Plenty under the floor does not put anything in the pot. It only makes the failure louder.

He did not build the machine and has never claimed to. Eight generations built it, a mile of drain and a footing at a time, each of them doing honest work to a pattern handed down, and he is the first man in all of it to have seen the whole and understood what it is for. What he added is a date.

How near it is: near. You would know it the way you know a bath has taken — by what stops happening. Hollow work is going out of Vessin. When everything shares water, no working is ever short with nothing near enough, because there is always something near enough; so it reaches instead of going hollow, and reaching at that thinness leaves no mark on anybody. Bread stopped coming out water and air about thirty years ago in the joined districts, and parish by parish since. Varo Aivemer has never in his working life seen a joint dust out in the first frost and thinks this is because masons are better now, and says so, and is liked for saying it. Anka Soolmer has not heard of a hollow wall in nine years and has begun lately to find that frightening without yet being able to say why. When the last parish is brought into the water, no setting anywhere in Vessin will ever go hollow again, and the country will call it the best age it has ever had, and it will be right about the bread.

### Event one: the Sootaavi Cut

**The schoolbook version.** In the hungry decade, when three wet summers rotted the grain and the wet hollows stood water till June, the parishes put their idle men to digging. Eleven years, four thousand spades, and a country that fed them out of common pots at the ditch-head every noon of it. The Cut drained the great hollow above Sootaavi and turned nine thousand acres of reed and fever into the best dairy pasture in Vessin. No one who worked on it went hungry, and no one has been hungry there since. It is the thing the Vessin point at when they are asked what kind of people they are, and they are not wrong to point at it.

**The detail that never fit.** The Cut runs the long way. It goes eleven miles round the shoulder of the down when it could have gone four straight, and children shown the two lines on a slate always ask why, and are told, correctly, that the straight way crosses hard chalk and would have taken twice as long. The other detail is smaller and nobody asks it at all: the ditches are floored. Drainage does not need a floor. Four thousand men spent two of their eleven years laying dressed stone in the bottom of a ditch whose whole purpose is to carry water away.

**What it actually accomplished.** A dam four hundred miles long, holding the seep against the chalk instead of letting it run out to the river — the long way round is the contour, and the contour is where you put a dam. It held the stock of eleven parishes still and started it moving, at the speed of grass, toward the low ground west-by-north. It also brought every one of those parishes' water into one running system for the first time, which is the reason the dairies are clean, the reason nobody drinks bad water, and the reason the country is one pot.

**The moment it flips.** August, a drought year, the Cut down to a trickle. Sevi Kestamer has walked out with Talu Rannmer to see the bottom, because in forty years the bottom has never shown. They stand in the ditch on cracked mud. The floor is dry-jointed slab, laid the short way, courses across the fall — the same pattern Sevi lays under her own vat, four courses of it, to keep her bath off the neighbour's ground. She has laid that pattern with her own hands and cursed the labour of it.

It is here at eighty times the size and it goes out of sight in both directions.

Talu is pleased. She says her great-grandmother's people worked nine years on this stretch and were fed for it every day. Sevi kneels and puts her flat palm on the slab, out of habit, the way you do.

A cool draught comes up out of it, in the third month of a drought, in a parish that has been worked all summer.

She keeps her hand there a while. What she says is, "It's very well laid."

### Event two: the Nateska downs

**The schoolbook version.** When the Sootaavi dairies filled and there was no more room in the valley, the country opened the pale downs at Nateska to anyone who would build there. Poor land, admittedly — pale land nobody had wanted for a century — but the country did not send people to it empty-handed. It gave stone for houses, and beasts, and, after four years and half the dressed stone in the district, eleven miles of elm pipe from the springs above Varenuor, so that a country of dry chalk would have sweet water at the door. Eight thousand went, of their own accord, inside one generation. They built well. Their grandchildren are still there and they keep the best troughs in Vessin.

**The detail that never fit.** They were given the water but not the wells. The lines were set out so that every house stands on the pale ground and every spring-head in the district lies outside the ground they were given, which was explained then and is explained now as a matter of the ground being sound to build on, and the ground is sound. And no setter has ever been trained at Nateska. The two or three born there in a century who had the hands for it were taken to Varenuor as children, to learn from somebody, and none of them ever came home to work. This is put down to the sort of people they are, or to the pull of a big crossing, and it is mentioned kindly.

**What it actually accomplished.** There is no stock under white ground, so no setting can be done on it or within sight of it. That is why nobody there ever learned the trade, why nobody there has ever felt a turn, and why nobody there knows what one feels like or would know it in the room. They are the one district in the country with no hands in it that could say stop. And the pipe from Varenuor put eight thousand well-fed, honestly-fed, unmended people into the pot of the whole country. They cannot draw. They can only be drawn from. They were not given the safest land in Vessin. They were put on it, at their own asking, with thanks, and the asking was the construction step.

The second thing the downs do is quieter and it is why the pipe went where it went. The level under a country tilts toward whatever has been taken low, the way a floor tilts toward a drain, and used-up ground never fills. So Nateska pulls at everything around it. The parishes on that side go down faster than their neighbours and always have, and their dyers complain of it, and Anka rests them oftener than the chalk should require, and this looks exactly like poor luck on poor ground. White ground does not spread by its own paling alone. It spreads by being next to.

**The moment it flips.** Neisa Vettamer has come out to mend a carter's boy's forearm and has spent twenty minutes on a floor that gives her nothing at all, which she expected, because everyone told her. The boy's mother is apologetic about it and offers her food she cannot take payment in. They will take him to Varenuor in the cart. They always do. It is a nuisance and it is nobody's fault.

Going out, Neisa stops at the trough by the pipe-head, which is full, in August, and cold. She has been hearing about it since she arrived; it is the pride of the place. The mother says it has run every day for ninety years, that they have never had a dry summer, that the country was good to them and they know it.

Neisa puts two fingers in it. It is the same water she washed her hands in at the crossing that morning, eleven miles back, and it will be in Sevi Kestamer's bath at Kelaavi by Thursday.

She is asked whether she will stay for supper. She says yes, and eats it.

### Who knows

Perhaps thirty people understand the whole of it, and never more than about forty at once. Nobody goes looking for them. They are noticed. A woman in her sixties who has walked a river for thirty years and has wondered all of them why the resting keeps favouring the same seep; a mason who has relaid four footings to a pattern that makes no sense and has gone looking for the fifth — these are people who have already done most of the work themselves, and one evening somebody older sits down opposite them at a table and finishes their sentence for them, and they say nothing for a long time. Nobody promises anything. It is a thing you are told once, by somebody older, and then you know it.

They keep quiet for the ordinary reason. The first thing you understand, once you can see the whole, is that the works feed the country now. Cut the water apart and hollow work comes back the same season: water bread, dusting walls, a bone of tallow in a smith's arm. Stop the drains and the hollows stand and the fever comes back into them inside two summers. Whatever the apparatus was built for, it is at present the reason nine thousand acres are pasture and nobody drinks bad water, and the people who know this are not monsters, and would tell you so at length, and would be telling the truth. Most of them have privately decided that the thing it was built for will never actually be done, and that their work is to keep the good half running and hand it on. One of them has kept the Sootaavi drains open for thirty-one years and has twice refused to let the sentence be finished.

Anka Soolmer is the third refusal. She was sat down opposite one autumn by a man she had known since she was twenty, and she saw where the sentence was going before he was four words into it, and she stood up and put her coat on and walked out into the dark and went home. She has told nobody. She has gone on walking her river and resting her parishes and being right, and she now knows two things at once: that there is a sentence, and that she has never once been able to make the resting come out any way but west-by-north.

And then there are the half-knowers, who are the good setters — the careful ones, thirty years in, who have noticed that the work has got easy. Nothing goes hollow any more. The pot is full wherever you kneel, whatever the parish, whatever the season, however hard the summer has been. They know exactly what a full pot means when the ground is worked out and the crock is empty, because they teach it to girls in sheds: good feel is bad news. They have felt the country turn kind, and they have worked out roughly what the kindness takes and roughly whose spoonful it is, and they have gone no further with the thought, because the next step is putting your hands in your lap for the rest of your life while people come to the door with broken arms.

## Part Four - The Seven

A risen is not a thing Halu invented and it is not a creature. It is the documented, unheroic accident that happens to any fool who sets on the dead, and every parish in Vessin knows the shape of it: the body warms and breathes and sits up inside a quarter of an hour, made of thin slices off everyone inside the steam, loosest first — their warmth, then their last few days, then the newest things in their heads, then the skill in their hands. Two gravediggers put a rope on it and walk it out at a rope's length to bare ground, and it goes quiet in an afternoon and cold by evening, and nobody says a name over it.

Halu's contribution is the room. He gets a body off gravediggers, or takes one off white ground where nothing rots, and he sets on it in a bare kitchen with the floor dry, the door shut, the water off and nothing living inside the steam of it but himself. So it reaches, as it always reaches, and there is nothing in reach but the one man standing there. His warmth is not available — that went into a cooling bath at Hlemeska thirty years ago — so it starts one below and takes in the order it always takes: a stretch of his last days, then whatever was newest in his head that night, then the skill in his hands. What stands up is warm and breathing and made of that and nothing else, wearing a stranger's face.

What comes up is always one of the seven jobs that have to be done around a working and are not the setting itself: the ground cleared, the hand sat down and fed, the time kept, the pot held, the used-up sent home, the work passed sound, and somebody afterwards to say what it took. That is not a taxonomy he chose. It is what is left in the top of a man who has spent forty years standing at the side of other people's workings and never once been allowed to put his own hands in the water.

Each is short in the same three ways, and the ways matter more than the faces. It is cold from the inside, as he is, so it has no warmth to give and cannot sit as a hand. It holds only the slices it was made of — no last meal, no fat, nothing loose lying on top — so anything that takes from it goes straight into the middle, and the stages that give an ordinary body a rung to catch at are rungs it does not have. Overdraw comes down a person a step at a time and gives you years to notice. It comes down one of these all at once.

And it goes on taking off every living thing that stands near it, for as long as it stands, without being asked and without knowing. That is the part Halu cannot get round, and it is why he cannot simply station them. He moves them: a fortnight in a place, never twice in the same year, out along the resting seasons, and a parish that has had one is a parish that had a poor winter and blamed the weather and was half right. Not one of them has lasted four years. He makes more, and the making is the only working in Vessin where he is the only thing in the room and nobody else gives anything at all.

The parishes name them for the job, the way you name a dog you did not choose. Not one of them can say its own name, which is how you tell.

#### 1. Ilma — the one who clears the ground

**What it is.** A wide, pleasant woman who comes into a village a fortnight ahead of the work and asks questions about your trade. How do you know your bath is ready. Who closed that wall, and who closed it the time before. She listens with her head tipped and gives you your own answer back an hour later like a present. People like her and are tired after her, and the fire wants building up when she goes. She keeps her hands in ash and lime and out of the light, and the backs of them are cracked open across the knuckles, because that was a habit before it was hers.

**What it does for him.** She makes the great workings possible at all. Dogs out, doors hung, floors dried, troughs emptied, the sluice above the village chocked shut so the leat is not running, and the whole parish told where to put its feet. She also finds the twice-mended houses, because a working drawn off mended material comes out hollow and Halu's sites have to come out sound. Every one of his three great workings stands on a fortnight of Ilma being thorough.

**Its premise.** *Clean the ground first. Nothing takes on dirty cloth.*

**Its death.** At Hlemeska she cleared a bake-house herself the night before, being thorough, because she always finished by testing her own work: the dog put out, the floor swept dry, the water off, the door shut, the two women who worked there sent home with bread. Then she did the small setting she always did to see whether a room was clean — a cup of milk thickened, nothing, a minute's work — and the stock was thin under that floor and the pot was short by a little, and there was nothing left inside the steam but her. She had no meal on her and no fat under it and no rung anywhere. It took four minutes. Talu Rannmer and Sevi Kestamer stood out in the yard on dry ground, the way they had been taught since they could walk, and Sevi did not go in.

**The refutation.** Emptying a room does not stop a short working; it settles who is left, and the last one left in a properly cleared room is the one who cleared it. The more scrupulous the clearing, the more certainly that is you.

**What it takes out of them.** Talu answered her, three times, over two weeks, about her own trade — which is how a risen learns where to take next. The days went with Ilma and did not come back when she stopped. Talu has lost the fortnight she was taught to set a bath and will have to learn it again from a woman who is now afraid of her.

#### 2. Hlusa — the one who sits the hand down

**What it is.** A neat old woman at the near end of the bench with bread and butter and salt on a board in front of her, who knows everyone's name and when they last gave and sends them home if it was this month. She will not let the house eat before the hand does. She cannot eat. She holds a slice for the length of a conversation and puts it back on the board. The house has learned to eat facing the fire.

**What it does for him.** She keeps the benches full without one person being made to sit. Fed first, worked slow, walked home, meat afterwards if there is meat — the courtesies are the whole reason a decent person sits down at a trough, and she keeps them herself, by hand, at whatever crossing he has put her at that season. Without her a bench is a queue of hungry people and anyone can see it.

**Its premise.** *Nothing holds unless something is put in that makes it hold. Put in the manners and the giving holds.*

**Its death.** At Kelaavi in a hard week the bench stood empty, because the parish had begun to refuse. Hlusa sat down on it herself, put her hand in the water to the wrist, and said go on. Sevi fed her first, correctly, and the food was held and not eaten. Sevi worked slow, correctly. It made no difference: there was no meal on Hlusa to come off the top and no fat under it, so it went straight into the slices she was made of. It took a little under an hour. She was polite the whole time and thanked Sevi twice. The wall closed sound, and it is the only sound wall in that village.

That same week, at the same trough, a carter's wife sat as a hand for the winter bread and had every courtesy done for her — fed first, worked slow, walked home in the dark, a bit of bacon on the Sunday. She had sat at that trough eleven times a year for six years, because it is the only place a woman with four children is fed for sitting still. By spring her teeth had gone. Hlusa did the courtesies over her on the Tuesday and sat down herself on the Thursday.

**The refutation.** Courtesy is not what makes a giving hold; a person with something loose in them is, and the manners had been keeping a bench full of people who had nothing loose left and hiding that fact from everybody including themselves. The courtesies govern the asking and have never once governed the taking.

**What it takes out of them.** Sevi worked a hand who was past giving, hour on, in a cold room, because the wall had to close — which is precisely what Vensa Kestamer did to Halu at Hlemeska for four hours by lamplight. It worked. Sevi cannot now hold the opinion that it never works.

#### 3. Riku — the one who keeps the time

**What it is.** A young man with a flat voice who says *go on* and does not appear to hear anything said back. He is fast at a bench and he cannot tell one hour from four; the days he is made of all felt the same from inside. He keeps time by making somebody count aloud for him, and he gets rough when nobody will.

**What it does for him.** He compresses. A district's whole season — bread, walls, bones, colour — run back to back in one afternoon on deep ground, with a bench of sixty and the parish watching. That is the visible part of Halu's argument made into an actual day of work, and people bear one afternoon a year who would not bear a taking spread across every day of it.

**Its premise.** *Faster is kinder. Get it over.*

**Its death.** Above Sootaavi, in a parish that had been worked hard three years running, he took a season in an afternoon off the same sixty. A bench works because a hand gives her dinner and goes home and eats double for two days and comes back in a month with something loose on her again. Run it end to end and there is no month. By the fourth hour the sixty had nothing on top of them, so the working stopped coming off the top of a bench and began coming out of the middle of a crowd, and Riku could not tell whether he was in the first hour or the fifth. The parish stopped him the only way they had: they stopped counting aloud. He had to break off in the middle of a working. It went flat, and the whole of that district's stock went into it and was spent regardless, and there was no bread. Then he pulled, because he could not feel that the floor was empty, and pulled again. Overdraw came down on a body with no taste to lose, no colour to lose and no warmth to lose, and no rung anywhere to catch at, and went the whole way at once. The crust came on him between noon and dark. He finished standing and stayed standing, entirely sensible, joints set, unable to turn his head, and could not have told you afterwards whether it had taken an hour or a week.

**The refutation.** Speed takes nothing off the whole of it; it removes the one thing that ever limited the taking, which is the month in which a hand fills back up. Sixty people who would have given sixty times off the top gave once out of the middle, and the man who could not tell one hour from four spent the whole of his own ruin inside one, awake, which is exactly what he had been doing to districts.

**What it takes out of them.** Sevi and Anka Soolmer walked him out themselves, on a rope, changing places at the near end so the taking was shared, because the parish would not go near him. They did not answer him. He used Talu's voice for two miles. And the district's work went undone: forty households went into the winter on honest bread and not enough of it, and Sevi is the reason there was any argument about it at all.

#### 4. Kirro — the one who holds the pot

**What it is.** A grey man who stands nearer to you than you want. He is cold to hold, no dye holds on him, cuts on him stay open for weeks, and workings go past him. He is barely in any room he is in. Talking to him is like standing in a doorway in March.

**What it does for him.** He is the pair of hands at the middle of everything. All of them are cold and none of them can give; only Kirro has the coldness as a property of the thing itself, because he was made in the one room where that slice was there to be taken. So the working goes past him to the flour, to the dog, to the girl by the door, and Kirro can hold the pot at every great working in the country and never once be the one it comes off. He is how Halu is present everywhere now that Halu cannot rise from a chair. For three years this genuinely saved people. At Kelaavi and at Hlemeska and twice at Sootaavi a working ran short and went past the man holding it and into the sacks, and the sacks are why nobody remembers those days as anything.

**Its premise.** *The taking should be done by the one it cannot touch.*

**Its death.** On the long span at Varenuor a working turned. Kirro could not feel it, because the turn is cold arriving sideways and he was cold already and always. A woman at the back felt it and shouted, and the crowd went back off the wet stone onto dry ground the way children are taught about ice, and it emptied in under a minute. Sevi stood at the trough end and shouted at him to take his hands off. He answered her — pleasantly, in somebody else's phrasing, with something he had taken off somebody that morning — and left them in the water. With the room emptied there was nothing else inside the steam. Cold holds back, so it was slow: a little over an hour, and he was still explaining at the half.

**The refutation.** Cold does not put you outside the pot; it puts you last in it, and last is a place in the order and last arrives. The answer to a working that turns has never been to be hard to take from — it is to feel it and get your hands off, one act, wanting a feel he had been made without and a judgement no slice of a man has ever contained. He could be told. He could not be warned.

**What it takes out of them.** Sevi put her arm in to pull his out and lost four days off the top of herself, including the only conversation in which Talu had ever asked her for anything. The span closed hollow that afternoon and it reads sound, and there are three hundred people a week on it.

#### 5. Nemi — the one who sends the used-up home

**What it is.** A thin, quiet woman who walks a parish looking at faces. She can tell by the eyes and the gums and the way a wrist sits who has nothing loose left in them, and she is right nearly always. She takes those people off the bench, kindly, and finds them other work. She can say every one of them, going back three years, in order, without a book and without hesitating.

**What it does for him.** She is the reason nobody starves. She keeps the used-up away from the troughs, which keeps the benches from visibly killing anyone, which keeps the country willing to sit down. Of the seven, hers is the work a decent person would be proud of, and she does it well.

**Its premise.** *Protect them by sending them away.*

**Its death.** Saying the names in order one evening, she heard what she was saying. It was the same forty people. They came back under one parish's name and then another's, because the bench is where a person with nothing is fed, and the ones with nothing come back, and she had been taking the same forty off the same bench for three years and calling it a hundred and twenty. She went to Halu at the great working above the Sootaavi crossing and told him the truth, which is that this is an empty district and it must not be worked. He agreed with her at once and thanked her. Then he applied her own rule to her and said, gently, that there was nothing left in her either, and that she should go and stand well off. She agreed with that too; she is a piece of him and can no more disagree than his hand can. She walked an hour, over the shoulder of the down and out of sight of the crossing, to the pale ground where nothing lives, and stood there. It took two days, and it took two days only because Talu Rannmer walked out and stayed with her and answered her, so that there was one living thing inside the steam of her to go on taking from.

**The refutation.** Knowing the names is not keeping anybody: she could say all of them in order and had never once made a bench shorter by one, and the list was the whole of what she did. Sending a person away is protection only when the place they go has something in it, which is proved by the fact that the only thing keeping her alive on that ground was somebody standing close enough to be taken from.

**What it takes out of them.** Sevi was in the room and said nothing, because Nemi going meant the working stopped for a day and forty people did not sit at the trough; she let it happen and knew what she was letting go for it. Talu came back off the down short another year, and does not regard this as having been a decision.

#### 6. Svaali — the one who passes the work sound

**What it is.** The newest and the warmest of them. A calm, capable man who lays his palms on a wall or a loaf or a span and says it is sound, and is believed, because his hands find a full pot everywhere and always. He passed the long bridge at Varenuor. He passed the winter bread of two districts. He is the country's confidence, and he is made of the skill in Halu's hands, which is the deepest thing there was left in him to take.

**What it does for him.** He is the one who says the work is finished. Without him the apparatus is a set of great workings nobody will stand near twice; with him, a district gives one afternoon a year and eats its bread and crosses its river and does not look underneath. Everything Ilma cleared and Riku compressed and Kirro held is worth nothing until Svaali puts his hands on it.

**Its premise.** *The hands know. If they say it is sound, it is sound.*

**Its death.** February at Kelaavi, the bench empty because the parishes had begun refusing, and the winter bread short. Sevi sat as his hand — for him, deliberately, to keep the working off the household through the wall — fed herself first, sat still, and made him work slow. Halfway through she felt the cold come sideways out of the room and told him to take his hands off. He put his own palms flat on the tub, felt plenty, and went on. So she lifted her hand out of the water. The pot was then short by exactly one person, and the only rich thing left inside the steam was the man standing in it with his hands in the water. In a whole man the hands are nearly the last thing to go, and there is a great deal above them. There was nothing above Svaali at all. It took him empty in the time a bath takes to go off heat. He sat down in the water and could not say what he had been for.

**The refutation.** No pair of hands passes its own work; his were the finest instrument in Vessin and they found a full pot at the exact moment they were the only thing left in it, which is what they had been doing on every span and every loaf for four years. The last thing a pair of hands is wrong about is itself.

**What it takes out of them.** Sevi sat as a hand for one of Halu's own, and the bread came out sound, and the parish ate it and thanked her — so she can no longer hold that a bench is only theft. And Talu watched her take her hand out of the water and watched a living thing go empty because of it, and has understood that it can be done on purpose, and wants to.

#### 7. Toma — the one who says what it took

**What it is.** A big, warm, likeable man who talks the way Halu talks: short sentences, one thing at a time, waiting after each to see whether you have it. He ropes off dry ground at every crossing he comes to, open and downwind, and tells the parish where to put its feet, and the ropes stay up after he has gone. At the end of a working he says aloud, in front of everybody, what was taken and off whom. He cannot set. He was made out of the speech and not out of the hands.

**What it does for him.** He makes the harm the size of a thing you can see. He is the best of Halu and knows it. He is the reason a district can watch its own afternoon and say the size of it out loud, instead of a country going a meal behind for two hundred years with nobody's name anywhere near it.

**Its premise.** *Say what it took and off whom, out loud, in front of everybody. Nothing that gets said out loud goes on for two hundred years.*

**Its death.** After the span turned, the crossing at Varenuor had to be worked again, in the open, on Toma's own roped ground, with the whole parish standing where he had told them to stand. Anka Soolmer counted aloud the way he had taught them all to count, and did not stop at the bench. She counted the sixty at the trough, and then the watchers, and then the watchers' children, and then she looked at the wet flags under two hundred pairs of feet and said what the steam would reach. Nobody went home. They stood exactly where he had put them, and put their hands in their pockets, and not one of them walked to the trough, and the setter Halu had sent — an old woman from Kelaavi who was doing as she was told and had been decent all her life — stood by a bench with nobody on it.

So Toma did the thing his own premise required. He said, in front of the parish, that the count was right and that the bread still wanted setting, and that there was one body on that ground that had nobody to speak for it. Then he sat down at the near end of the bench and put his wrist in the trough and told her to go on. She went on. He had nothing loose on him anywhere. It took an afternoon, and he talked for most of it, and the parish heard the whole of what it took, off whom, out loud, which was what he had been built for. Nobody touched him. The bread came out sound. They did not say a name over him, and he had none to say.

**The refutation.** Saying a thing out loud makes it refusable, and refusable is not the same as refused; the first competent count his own instrument ever produced was a count of him, and the answer it gave was that the cheapest body on the ground was the one holding the instrument. And the two things actually killing that parish were never in anybody's count: the hollow span under their feet, which reads sound and has three hundred people a week on it, and the ground going the colour of dry straw in a long tongue downhill and west-by-north of the crossing, following the seep and not the working, which is why the farmers argue about the weather and are not stupid to.

**What it takes out of them.** Anka counted honestly and a district's winter bread was set that afternoon by a frightened old woman off a bench with one man on it, and Anka has to hold both of those. And among Toma's slices was the newest thing in Halu's head the night he made him, which was his mother's face over a bench at Varenuor — she sat there for whoever asked, in the years when they needed the loaf — and when Toma went quiet, that was the last of what anybody living remembered of her.

### The Rising Argument

1. Emptying the room does not stop the taking; it settles who is left to be taken from, and that is the one who emptied it.
2. The courtesies do not make a giving hold; a person with something loose in them does, and the manners hide which is which.
3. Speed does not reduce what is taken; it removes the month in which a hand fills back up, and puts the whole of it in one afternoon.
4. Being hard to take from is not being exempt; last is a place in the order, and last arrives.
5. Sending the used-up away is protection only where the place they go has something in it; otherwise it is only sending them away, and knowing all their names has never once shortened a bench.
6. Hands cannot pass their own work, because the last thing a pair of hands is wrong about is itself.
7. Saying the size of a harm out loud makes it refusable, not refused — and leaves every harm nobody can see exactly where it was.

Seven ways of standing outside the taking, all seven of them pieces of one man's answer to it, and not one of them able to do the only thing that has ever stopped a working: be a whole person, feel it, and stop.

Nothing comes back when one of them goes quiet. The days, the habit and the skill in it are gone out of the world and not back into Halu — material travels into a pot and never out of one, and that holds for him as it holds for everybody. So each death takes a function off him for good. He will not get his manners back, or his timing, or his mother, or his hands. He is a crusting man in a warm chair who cannot walk to a bench, and he has fewer of them every year, and every one he makes costs him another stretch of what is left in the top of him.

He goes on making them, and his reason is the best one he has: it is the only working in Vessin where he is the only thing in the room, and nobody else gives a spoonful.

## Part Five - The Small Atrocity

### The situation

Ruvo Rannmer is kicked by a plough-horse on the second frost of the year and comes back to the yard on a hurdle with the shin broken in two places and the leg turned wrong below the knee. He is nineteen, a ploughman with a good singing voice and no conversation. It is eleven miles to anyone else, the ground is hard, and the light is going.

When they lifted him onto the hurdle he brought his dinner up in the yard. Four people saw him do it and not one of them thought anything of it, because that is what a man does when a horse has broken his leg.

The other way is a splint. Everyone in the byre has watched a splinted leg go bad in cold weather — the swelling, and then the fever, and then the choice between a man with one leg in a country where every trade is standing work, or a man in the ground by the new year. Neisa Vettamer, who is the setter for Hlemeska and two parishes past it, has closed this kind of break forty times. She says she can close it. Nobody in the room argues, because the argument is not worth having.

### The people

**Neisa Vettamer** is twenty-six, wide-handed and slow-spoken, and has already closed forty of these because there is nobody else in three parishes and there has not been for six years. She has never lost anybody. She stays the second night without being asked, sleeping in her coat by the fire so the family can sleep. She says *I don't know* readily, which in that country is the whole of a good name.

She has one apprentice, a girl of fifteen who is not there that night, and the thing she has told the girl most often is this: *mended material reads full. Your hands will tell you a whole pot when the pot is water and dust, and they will tell you in the same voice they tell you the truth in. That is the whole of the trap and there is no signal on it.* The girl can say it back. So could Neisa, at fifteen.

**Liva** is thirty-four and is called Liva of the Kelaavi road, because her mother's house lost its rushes and its name in the same summer and she has brought nothing to the pot since. She scours vats for the dye-house by the day and carries milk up the hill for the Rannmers, and she wants a goat of her own, because a goat is a material and a material is halfway back to a name. She takes a pinch of salt out of every crock she scours, which everyone knows and nobody mentions.

**Meiva Rannmer**, Ruvo's sister, stands out in the yard in the cold for two hours with her back to the byre door, because that is what you are taught to do.

### The procedure

It is done four or five times a winter at a hearth like this one. It is done every day of the week on the benches at the crossings, which is a different thing, and nobody in Vessin has ever thought of the two as the same thing at all.

The floor is checked dry and the straw pulled back to bare stone. The dog goes out. The door is shut and the shutter dropped, because an open door lengthens the reach downwind. Neisa puts her palm flat on the byre floor and waits. Hlemeska is wet ground and the draught comes up cool and steady, so there is plenty under her. That is the easy half and it is the half people think of.

Then the hand. You ask; you do not send for one and you do not tell a woman she is doing it. You ask whether she has given this month, and if she has you thank her and go short instead. You feed her first, and nobody in the house eats until she has: bread and butter and salt, all she will take, because the working goes for the newest meal on her before it goes for anything that is hers. The salt is not for the taste. A bone wants mineral, and the loosest mineral in any room is in the last thing somebody ate; after that it is in their teeth. She sits on your left with her hand in the water to the wrist and holds still. You work slow, so the taking comes off the top of her. Afterwards the house gives meat if the house has meat, and you walk her home yourself and see her indoors.

And you say it before you start, flat, twice through, the way it is said to children learning to stir, in the shape the bonesetters have worn it into: *nothing's in the bone that wasn't in the man, and the warmth comes up out of the floor.*

### The scene

Neisa asked Liva four things at the door. She believed all four answers and all four were true.

"Have you eaten today?"

"Cheese at noon and again at four," Liva said. "I've had a good week."

"Have you sat for anyone this month?"

"Not since haytime."

That was true. She had sat nine days before at the bench at Varenuor with her hand in the trough for most of a morning and been given a coin, but that was the bench, and the bench is work, like turning a windlass.

Neisa knew the bench was there. Everybody in three parishes knows the bench is there.

"Not the bench either?"

"That's not sitting for anyone," Liva said, surprised to be asked.

"No," said Neisa. "It isn't."

"Are you willing?"

"For meat I am."

So Liva ate first and the rest of them watched her do it, which is the courtesy and is never comfortable. She had bread and butter and a great deal of salt and would have stopped at half of it if Neisa had not kept putting it in front of her. "Go on. All of it. You'll thank me."

"I'll thank the butter."

Ruvo lay on the door they had carried him in on, with his boot cut off and a strap of harness between his teeth and his eyes shut. Neisa knelt on the bare stone by his shin with the basin between them and Liva's hand in the water, and asked him the question she asks strangers' walls.

"Was this leg ever closed before?"

"Broke it as a boy," Ruvo said around the strap. "Seven."

"Closed, I said. Did a setter put her hands on it?"

"My aunt splinted it. Six weeks in the loft and a stick after." He thought about it. "There wasn't a setter come to us."

Out in the yard Neisa asked Meiva the same thing, because you ask twice and you ask somebody who was standing up at the time, and Meiva gave her the family's answer, which is the answer the family has given for twelve years: the year before the bad winter, the aunt, the splint, the loft. Meiva was four. She is repeating a thing her aunt told her, and her aunt told it that way because she had been ashamed to send for the setter in a winter when the whole village was sending for her, and had said so once at a table, and had gone on saying it until it was simply what had happened.

It was the bad winter. Everybody in Hlemeska who broke anything that winter had it closed, by a woman twenty years dead who had a whole village short and nothing to close it with, and who did it anyway, and was thanked. The bone had knitted straight and had carried Ruvo Rannmer for twelve years, and under Neisa's palms it read the way sound bone reads.

"All right," said Neisa, and set her hands either side of the break, and said the words, flat, twice through. Meiva, in the yard, heard her voice through the door and not the words.

It came on smooth and slow. Not eager — she would have taken her hands off for eager. Just steady and even and unhurried, the way the good ones come, and no cold out of the room at all, and Liva's hand still in the water where she had put it.

Neisa worked the length of the shin twice over and did not hurry. Twice she thought it was taking a long while for a young man's leg, and twice she put that down to a bad break in two places.

"You're all right?"

"Cold," said Liva.

"That's it working. Not long."

It was another quarter of an hour. When Neisa took her hands away the shin under the skin was straight and whole and the right length, the split lip of the wound had closed to a red seam, and Ruvo had gone to sleep.

Liva took her hand out of the basin and it shook, and then the whole of her shook, and she laughed at it, which people do. Her lips had gone the colour of the water.

"There," said Neisa. "Sit."

"I'm hungry enough to eat the door."

"You will. Meiva — meat."

Meiva came in with the cold on her and cut what they had, which was a wedge of bacon meant for the week, and Liva ate it and then ate bread with the fat, and drank the water out of the basin because it was there and Neisa let her, since there was nothing left in it that had not come out of the room. Colour came back into her mouth. Ruvo snored. The byre smelled of cows and iron.

At the door Neisa put Liva's arm through hers and they went up the lane slowly, stopping twice, and neither of them said anything about stopping. Liva had the crust of the loaf in her apron.

"That's for my landlady's hens," she said, "before you say anything."

"I've said nothing."

"You were going to."

At the house Neisa waited outside until the rushlight went up in the window, and then went back down the hill to sleep in her coat by the Rannmers' fire, as she does.

### Why it is correct

There was plenty under the floor all evening and the working never went near flat. The material was the trouble. A leg wants mineral, and the bone in front of her was not bone the whole way through: a good part of what her hands were reading as bone had been closed once out of an empty pot, in a winter when there was nothing to close it with, and was tallow and water — honestly made, twelve years sound, and empty.

So the shortfall was large, and it went where a shortfall goes when a hand is sitting in the pot: down through what was loosest, taking each thing empty before it moved on. Liva's meal first, which is why she was fed. Then Ruvo's fat, because a fed ploughman has fat and fat is loose. Then Liva's, and she had almost none. Then the water and the blood, and then the teeth, because by that point the only mineral left standing loose in that byre was in two people's mouths and one of those people had a strap between his teeth and the other had her wrist in the basin.

There was no turn, because the turn is what reaching feels like, and this was not reaching. She had given her hand on purpose. Giving does not announce itself. It comes on smooth and slow and even, which is precisely what sound work feels like, and there is no third thing it could have felt like instead.

Neisa asked whether the leg had been closed and was told the truth twice by two people repeating a story an ashamed woman told at a table when they were children. She asked whether Liva had given this month and was told the truth by a woman who does not think of the bench as giving, because nobody does, because there is nobody there it is given to. She fed her first. She worked slow. She gave meat. She walked her home. There is no step she left out and none she could have added.

### The afterwards

Ruvo Rannmer is three weeks in bed and comes out of it a stone lighter and grey about the face, and everybody says that is the leg, and by haytime he is ploughing and singing and is exactly the man he was.

Liva sleeps fourteen hours and eats double for three days and is not right by the end of the week, which she puts down to the season. Her hands ache in cold water, which is unfortunate in a woman who scours vats. In December her gums bleed on hard bread and she starts soaking the crust. In March a back tooth comes out on a heel of bread with no pain at all, and she looks at it in her palm for a while and then goes on eating. She stops carrying two pails up the hill and starts carrying one, twice, which takes longer, so she gets fewer hills in a day, so she earns less, so she eats less. She is cold now in a way she was not cold before. Nobody notices any of it, Liva least of all, who experiences it as the ordinary business of getting older, having got older, which she has.

In April, at the spring, Neisa Vettamer sees her filling a pail and says the kind thing.

"You did that well in the winter. Steady as a post. I'd have you again, and I don't say that of everyone."

Liva is pleased for a week. She sits again in June, at the bench at Varenuor, because it is a coin, and in September at the Kestamers' for a dye-vat, because it is meat.

She does not bleed for the better part of a year, and tells nobody, because there is nobody it would be useful to tell.

### Why nobody is blamed

There is nobody to blame. Neisa did every part of it correctly and would do the same again on the same answers. Ruvo told her what his family had told him. The woman whose empty work is under all of it has been dead twenty years and was, at the time, keeping alive the people who are now the older half of the village. Liva was asked, and fed, and given meat, and thanked, and walked home, and said yes.

## Part Six - The Righteous Enemy

### Who she is

Svela of the Varenuor road, fifty-eight, a setter. The name is the insult and she wears it out loud. Her grandmother came up onto the pale downs at Nateska on a cart at nine years old, ninety years ago, when the country opened that ground to anyone who would build on it and gave stone for houses, beasts, and eleven miles of elm pipe from the springs above Varenuor, so that eight thousand people on dry chalk would have sweet water at the door. Eight thousand went, of their own accord, inside one generation, and they built well, and they are proud of it still. But there is no madder on the pale downs and no herd worth a rennet-house, and the material a family brings is the material its ground grows. Two houses in three put their name down at the edge of that country and came up onto it with nothing, and were called after the road they came by, and their grandchildren are called it yet. Svela says it first, flat, before anyone else can: *of the Varenuor road. My house brings nothing.*

The saying at Nateska is that no setter is ever born there, and it is nearly true. The two or three in a century who have the hands are found by accident, eleven miles off, in a cart. Svela was taken to Varenuor at eleven with a green-stick arm, the way every Nateska child with a broken anything is taken, and the old woman who mended it kept her, because the girl asked the right question on the second day: why does the floor at home feel like nothing? Nobody at Nateska had been able to tell her. Nobody at Nateska had ever felt a turn.

She is a small woman with a trough-keeper's shoulders and clean hands. She was a good bonesetter for thirty years and is still a very good one; she can put a collarbone right in a farm kitchen with two people watching and no fuss at all.

### What was done to her

She did not walk any road. What she survived is not the building of the thing but the thing it was built to do, done small, once, by accident, in her mother's yard.

She was eleven, home for the spring, standing on the kerb of the trough at the pipe-head holding the lamp while her mother Rovi swept the wrist-deep end, which was Rovi's work and her mother's before her, and which Nateska is proud of. It was March after a bad winter and the whole country was working: every crossing, oven and dye-house from the chalk to the sea, each of them a little short, none of them short by more than a handful. A shortfall less than a handful reaches with no turn at all. It feels like clean work.

Here is the part every setter gets wrong. The order a working takes things in is not a list of creatures; it is a list of how loose a thing is. Loose is what has just been eaten and not yet made into anything. A dinner an hour old in a man's stomach is looser than a hedge, looser than a standing crop, looser than a living sheep, and it always was — which is why a hand is fed first, and why nobody had ever thought the same sentence at the size of a country.

Over eight days, fifty-odd honest settings in four parishes each went looking for the loosest thing joined to them by water. The loosest thing in Vessin that March was eight thousand well-fed, honestly fed, unmended people at the far end of a pipe, who had never in their lives learned what cold arriving sideways meant, because there is no stock under white ground and so no setter was ever raised there to teach them.

It took the newest meal off everybody, which nobody notices. Off the ones with nothing loose under that, it went on down: the fat, and then the water and the blood, and then the teeth. Rovi went cold that spring and stayed cold, sat by the fire in a hot August four years running, and died of it at fifty. Sonna, Svela's sister, was carrying twins. Anywhere else in Vessin a midwife would have said the flat sentence they say — *you do not ask a woman carrying, she has nothing loose in her and it goes to her blood* — but nobody was asking anybody anything at Nateska, because nobody at Nateska knew there was a question. Sonna lost her teeth by the summer and her milk in a fortnight. One child died at eleven days. Talvi, who lived, is thirty-one now, cannot lift a full pail, and never could.

Nine people at Nateska died that spring. The parish called it a fever and was sorry about it.

Nobody did anything wrong. Every setter in those four parishes worked with a full crock and a good bath and hands that came up full, because the hands read the room and that spring the room was the whole of Vessin. Svela has since found three of them. Two are dead. The third is a kind old man at Sootaavi who taught half the girls in his district for nothing, and who cried when she explained it to him, and she let him. That is the part she cannot put down: there is nobody to hate at the end of it, the pipe is still cold and full in August, and it is still the pride of the place.

### Her rule

*I take only from people who can feel the turn — the ones who sit for me and the ones I go after, both. If you have never felt one you are not in my pot, and I will go short before you are.*

It has cost her twice over. At the crossing below Kelaavi she had a working laid out and a night's start on the people she was hunting, and the only body in the house that would have sat for her was a carter's lad of nineteen, well fed and willing, who would have put his wrist in the water gladly for a supper and a story. She sent him out to the yard, worked short, and the setting went flat — nothing changed, the stock under that floor was spent regardless and gone until the turn of the year, and she lay in the straw listening to them cross at dawn.

It also makes her a poor hunter of the only people who matter. She can go after two women in the whole country. She cannot touch a mason, or a woman who keeps a leat, or a woman who walks a river and decides which parish rests — and those three are the ones with their hands actually on the thing. She knows this. She says it is the only honest handicap in Vessin and that she would rather lose with it than win without it, and she is going to lose with it.

She has come near breaking it once, in a shut warm kitchen at Hlemeska in December with her wrists in the bath and the working rising eager and fast and better than she had hoped, which is the worst news a pair of hands can give you. She knew exactly what the plenty was. It was standing in the doorway with wet feet holding a lamp, twelve years old, watching. What stopped her was not conscience. It was the mother saying, in the ordinary voice you would use about ice, *step back onto dry ground* — the sentence every child in Vessin is told and nobody at Nateska ever had cause to say. Svela took her hands out of the water and lost the night.

### Who pays for her

Setters, and only setters, and only the careful ones thirty years in who have already worked most of it out for themselves — the ones who have noticed that nothing goes hollow any more and have gone quiet about it. She finds them the way anybody finds them, by asking who closed the wall.

She tells them the whole of it before they sit down: what the water is, what it was joined for, what the pipe at Nateska does, who she is hunting, and that they may well think her wrong and sit anyway. Then she does it properly, which is the unbearable part. Bread and butter and salt first, all they will take, and nobody eats before the hand. She works slow, so it comes off the top of them and not out of the middle. Meat afterwards if she has meat, and she walks them home, and if one of them has given this month she sends her away and goes short herself. They go home cold and shaking and ravenous, sleep twelve hours, eat double for two days and are right by the end of the week.

What they get is the truth, which several of them have wanted for twenty years and could not get out of their own trade, and which she is the only person in Vessin who will hand over without wanting anything back. That is what she gives them, and what it takes out of them, and both of them know it before the water is warm. Two of her hands have since stopped setting altogether and put their fingers in their laps for good, and their villages now send for someone worse. She knows their names and both villages. She does it again.

### Why she hunts the protagonists

Not because she has them wrong. Because she has them right. They mean to stop the man and leave the works in the ground, and their reasons are good ones — cut the water apart and hollow work comes back the same season, water bread and dusting walls and standing water in the hollows by June — and Svela can recite those reasons better than they can.

She simply notices what the reasons decide: that the pipe stays; that Nateska stays in the pot; and that the people at the far end of it will go on giving a spoonful at a time for another hundred years without once being asked, because being asked is a thing that only happens to people who can feel the turn.

### Her best scene

The trough at the pipe-head at Nateska, August, full and cold, in a country three months into a drought. Ruska Aivemer, sixty-one, a setter of forty years from Sootaavi, sits on the kerb with her wrist in the water. She has been fed. She does not speak.

There is no stock under that ground and there never has been. No working of any kind can be done there or in sight of it, and Svela has brought a woman eleven miles up onto white chalk to sit as a hand at a trough where nothing can be set, for the single reason that Sevi Kestamer will have to stand and look at her.

"Put your hand in. Ninety years it's run. My mother swept this end."

"I know what it is," Sevi says.

"You know what it is and you're going to leave it. Say that part out loud, at least."

"If I cut the water apart, the bread goes hollow this season. In every parish at once. Hlemeska's forty, everywhere."

"Yes. And they'll be standing in their own kitchens with their own setters, and by autumn every one of them will know what a hollow loaf smells like and be watching for it. Mine never got that. Mine got a fever, and a kind word, and nine graves."

"That's not a small thing you're weighing against."

"I'm not weighing. I'm counting. Ruska here can pull her wrist out any time she likes and go home and eat my supper. I told her what I meant to take and where it would come off her, and she put it in the water. That's the whole difference between me and the country, and it isn't a small difference, it's the only one there is."

"You've hurt people who never laid a stone."

"I've hurt setters. Every one of them could have felt me coming. Name me a rule I keep that yours keeps."

Sevi does not have one, and says so, and then says the honest thing, which is: "If you cut it, people die who didn't get asked either."

"That's true," Svela says. "You can stop me. You can't answer me. Do the first and don't pretend it's the second."

### How she ends

Not beaten and not turned. She is taken once, and let go, and is back at it inside a month. When the water is cut apart she gets what she wanted and the cost lands exactly where everyone said it would, and she does not recant, does not gloat, and does not thank anybody.

At the close she is at Nateska, fifty-nine, mending what can be mended on ground that gives her nothing, going to Varenuor in the cart like everyone else. Two districts send for her now when a risen stands up, because she will take the near end of the rope and will not answer it, whatever voice it uses. She is very good at it. She has never said she was right, and she has never once said she was wrong.

### What the story never gives her

No one with a name ever apologises, because no one with a name did it; fifty decent people were short by less than a handful. She never learns which working took her mother, because there is no way to find out and nothing to find. And nobody on the protagonists' side ever tells her she was right — not at the end, not privately, not in the last chapter. The apparatus is defended by competent people doing good work, and a grievance that is acknowledged is a grievance that closes, and this one is not allowed to close, because the thing it is about is still running.

## Part Seven - The Refused Shortcut

### The position

The country is joined. The last leat at Hlemeska went in two springs ago and there is now no crossing, oven or dye-house in Vessin whose water does not run, by some path, into the water of every other. Nothing has gone hollow in nine years. Anka Soolmer walks her river and finds the pot full wherever she kneels, and has stopped saying so out loud.

Halu Aivemer will set on the dead at Varenuor on the last Thursday in August, in the third month of the drought that showed Sevi Kestamer the bottom of the Sootaavi Cut in June. He will do it in the open, at the crossing where three parishes came to watch the centring struck. The body is Vensa Kestamer's, four days dead — the setter who sat him in a cooling bath at thirty-eight and went on working him for four hours because he asked her to. He says he chose her because a parish will take a proof from a woman they knew.

It is a proof, and he has said so, and this is the part the story must let him have. He has stated the size of the spoonful out loud in advance: one meal and one night's sleep off every living soul in Vessin, which is less than a hand gives at a bench on a Tuesday, and nobody will feel it. If a country gives that and a woman stands up, then the bench is finished forever, and what replaces it is one afternoon a year in daylight where three parishes can see the whole size of the thing, with no house sitting twice in a season and the district that gives fed for a week by the district that did not. He has already run six years of it at Sootaavi. The bench there is gone, the coin is gone, the woman who used to sit eleven times a year sits once and eats for a week after, and the children coming up are taller than the next parish's, and every soul in Sootaavi will tell you so before you have got your coat off.

Sixty people are coming to sit at the rail with their wrists in the trough. They have been promised bread and a week's meat and they are the same sixty who are always at the rail. The parish will stand on the bridge and watch, which is the entire point.

His knuckles have set. His neck turns less each spring. His elbows do not straighten and he is carried in and seated before the doors open. West-by-north of his three great sites the grass has gone the colour of dry straw in a long tongue that follows the seep and not the working, and the farmers argue about the weather and are not stupid to.

Sevi Kestamer is fifty-one and has the best hands in the west of the country. She has spent thirty years wanting nothing more complicated than to be left alone in a shed with a good red, and the flaw the world is about to press on is that she knows exactly how good her hands are and has always been quietly pleased about it. She has knelt on the floor of the Sootaavi Cut in a drought and felt a cool draught come up out of it. She knows what lies under Varenuor, she knows what that trough is joined to, and she knows that a setting on the dead always reaches, because the thing a corpse is short of is not a substance and cannot be brought or stood near.

If nobody does anything, the working will reach, and what it reaches for is everything living that shares water with that trough, which is everyone.

### The shortcut

Put her hands in the water and take the working off him.

Reaching takes the loosest thing first and takes it empty before it moves on, and touching is surest. Sixty fed people with their wrists in that trough are nearer and looser than a nation joined by eleven miles of elm pipe. If the working is pulled hard and fast — not slow, not off the top of them, but straight down into the middle — the shortfall is met at the rail and never gets past it. The country beyond gives nothing, because the country is never needed. Halu cannot pull fast; his joints have set. Sevi can.

Who gives, and in what order: sixty, their last meal, then their fat, then their water and blood, then their teeth. The thin ones have no fat and go from the meal to the blood in about a minute. They will go home shaking and ravenous, most will be short for good, and perhaps nine will die before spring of nothing anybody can name. Then a risen stands up, and it is roped and stood well off from, and it goes quiet by evening, as they always do.

What it buys: the drains stay in. The bread keeps rising. Nobody drinks bad water. Hollow work stays gone. And Halu is finished in front of three parishes, having promised a spoonful from everybody and needed sixty out of the middle.

Anka Soolmer argues for it, and Anka Soolmer has stopped three villages from working themselves pale and is prouder of that than of anything else in her life.

"I have rested Kelaavi twice with a room of dyers screaming at me, and I did it by deciding whose summer was spoiled. That is the trade. Sixty, fed, on a bench, in daylight, who came for it — I have done worse than that on a Tuesday, for less."

"You are not choosing between sixty and nobody. Nobody was never one of them."

"Say what happens at Hlemeska in February when the bread goes back to water. Say it out loud with a number in it. You can't. I can. And say what comes up out of the hollows at Sootaavi when the drains slow — it will kill more people in six years than the bench has killed in sixty, and they will be small children and old women, and not one of them will have sat down to anything."

Sevi has no answer to that and does not pretend to have one.

### The refusal

The bridge is hot by ten. It is Halu's own bridge, the long span he closed himself, the thing he is most admired for, and it went hollow the afternoon he closed it and reads sound and has three hundred people a week on it. Sevi knows that and stands three parishes on it anyway, because she needs them out of the wet, and it is one of the several things she does not get to feel good about afterwards.

The leat is down to a hand's depth and the long trough has gone green at the waterline, and the crowd stands on the flags where the water has been sloshed out from the beasts all morning, because wet stone is cooler and they have been standing since eight. Wet stone is also water, and water is one thing wherever it lies, and every one of them is in the pot and does not know it.

Vensa is in the trough with the water at her collarbones and her hair floating. The sixty are on the bench along the rail with their sleeves rolled, wrists in, elbows dry. On a board at the trough-head there is bread and butter and a dish of salt, and nobody has touched it, because nobody eats before Halu does.

Two men lift him out of the chair and hold him while he stands and eats it, all he will take, in front of everybody. He has done this before every working for thirty years. It takes about two minutes. Sevi has come up the bridge with Talu Rannmer and Varo Aivemer and she watches how it is done: which arm each man takes, how long it takes to seat him again, how the coat is cut so the elbows do not show.

He sees her and is pleased. He asks after her bath and whether the drought has spoiled her water. He asks her to eat. She says no thank you.

He puts his forearms into the trough to the elbow and begins.

It comes up out of the ground under Varenuor, which is the deepest ground in the country and has been filling since before anyone's great-grandmother, and it comes up through him, and the water over Vensa goes still in the way water does.

Sevi walks out to the middle of the bridge, past the rail, into the crowd. She kneels down on the wet flags and puts her flat palm on the stone.

She can have it. That is the thing nobody watching knows and the thing she will not be able to explain afterwards to anyone who was not kneeling. It is coming up through the stone into her palm, all of it, the deepest ground in Vessin, and she is a better pair of hands than the man in the trough and always has been, and taking a working off a setter who cannot pull fast is not difficult, it is only quick. She can feel where it would go. She can feel the sixty on the rail the way you feel the weight of a wet cloth before you lift it. Her hand wants to close.

She keeps the palm flat and does nothing with it, which is the hardest work her hands have ever been asked to do, and there is no one in the world who sees her do it.

What the crowd sees is a woman kneeling to a working, which is the oldest thing in the country. You step back onto dry ground, and you do it before you have finished thinking, the way children elsewhere are taught about ice. A man in front of her steps back and treads on the woman behind him. She lifts a child off the wet and moves. The ripple goes out from Sevi in both directions along the bridge and off both ends of it, and three parishes are standing on dry stone and dry grass inside a minute and a half, and several of them are annoyed about it. The two men who carry Halu step back with everybody else. They are on the dry before they know they have moved.

Talu Rannmer walks the rail with her weed-hook and lifts wrists out of the water one after another, working left to right, saying *up*, and *up*, and *up*, and does not explain, and they come up, because she is the woman who keeps the leat and you do what she says at the water.

One man goes forward. Vaano Lammer, whose sister is fourth from the end of the bench, gets as far as the trough-head with the whole of his parish watching, and Talu puts the hook across his chest and holds him there with both hands. He is right by every ordinary standard of decency in that country and she never says a word to him, because saying it would take longer than the man has. He does not speak to her again as long as she lives.

Below, at the channel head, Varo Aivemer takes out four stones with a bar. Dry-jointed, laid the short way, no lime — his own trade's pattern, worked to for four hundred years, the pattern he has cursed at every footing he ever relaid because a course you can lift with a bar is a course you have to lift again. The leat's water turns aside into the chalk beside the abutment. The trough goes down to a shallow puddle at the low end, where Vensa lies and where Halu's forearms are, and between that puddle and the water of Vessin there is a foot and a half of dry stone.

Sevi stays kneeling with her hand flat. She says, "Take your hands out."

Halu says, "How do you know when a bath is ready?"

She does not answer him.

The cold arrives sideways, out of the room, in the third minute after the stones come up. Sevi feels it in her palm on the flags and so does every setter standing off in the grass, and nobody moves, because going in would put them in the pot and feed it, and they have known that since they could walk.

He feels nothing. He sold it.

After a while he says, in the same flat voice, "Has it turned?"

Sevi says, "Yes."

"How long?"

"Nine minutes."

He looks at his own hands in the puddle. They are the colour of tallow now. The cracks across the knuckles, which have stayed open for weeks at a time for thirty years, have stopped weeping. He tries to lift his forearms clear of the stone and his elbows do not straighten, and the two men who have carried him into every room in Vessin for twenty years are ten feet off on dry flags, and they love him, and they would come if he called.

At the quarter hour Vensa's body warms and breathes and opens its eyes and sits up in the drained trough.

It says, "Go on."

It says it in his voice, flat, one thing at a time, waiting after to see whether you have it. It has his phrasing and the knowledge in his hands. It looks at the bread on the board and reaches for it and cannot make the fingers work, because his fingers have not worked for four years. It does not say her name. It does not say his. Nobody answers it.

Halu watches it for some time.

"Then she was never in there at all," he says.

Nobody answers that either.

He works it through out loud, the way he explains a kiln to a nephew, one thing at a time, and gets to the end of it, and the end of it is that a bigger pot would have changed the size of the taking and nothing else, and that he has been wrong since he was thirty-eight years old in a cold shed with his arms in a bath.

Then he does not call out to the two men.

"You should have let me find it out with the country in it," he says. "Then everyone would know."

His eyes are going. He says, "Has anybody eaten?"

Sevi says, "No."

"Well," he says. "Let them eat."

He goes quiet before the hour is out, seated, arms in an empty trough, which is roughly how he has been carried into every room in the country for twenty years.

The two gravediggers arrive at two with a rope, because Sevi sent for them at eight that morning. They put it on the risen and walk it out at a rope's length onto the white chalk above the crossing, changing places at the near end so the taking is shared between them. It goes quiet in the afternoon and cold by evening and they bury it where it stopped, and nobody says Vensa Kestamer's name over it.

Varo comes up off the ladder with the key stone in his arms and asks whether he is putting it back. Sevi takes it off him and carries it down the bridge and out to the cart.

### Why it lands

His whole life rests on one claim: that the old settings on the dead came out wrong because a farmhouse kitchen has four people in it, and that shortfall is therefore a question of size. Make the pot big enough and the taking is a spoonful, and a spoonful is not a harm.

The refusal does not argue with that. It removes the size. Nobody sets anything against him. Nobody pushes. The only working on that bridge all afternoon is his own. Sevi takes the room out of the pot with a reflex taught to toddlers, and Varo takes the water out of it with four stones his own trade laid to be lifted, and then they stand off and let the thing he built do the thing it does.

It cannot go flat: the ground under Varenuor is the deepest in the country and two hundred years of stone drain-floor put it there. It cannot go hollow: what a corpse is short of is not a substance, so there is nothing in the pot to make the missing part out of. So it reaches, and reaching takes the loosest living thing it can get at and takes it empty before it moves on.

By then the only living thing it can get at is the setter. The stock comes up through him and out through his palms; material travels into a pot and never out of one; his own body was never available to be given, only to be reached for. That is the accident every apprentice is warned about in her first week, and it almost never happens, because there is always something looser in the room than the setter. Empty the room and the accident is the only thing left.

He cannot save himself, and the reasons are the two things he cut out of himself and the one thing they cost him. The whole of the answer to the turn is to get your hands off, and he cannot feel the turn, so he has no reason to believe a woman who tells him it has come. He sold the third stage before he ever pulled hard, so the crust came on him twenty years early and set his elbows and his knees, and he has not risen from a chair unassisted since he was forty-eight. And the men who lift him stepped back onto dry ground when a woman knelt, because they were taught to before they could talk.

He could still have been pulled out at the quarter hour. Two men who love him were ten feet away and would have come for one word. What stopped the word was the thing sitting up in the trough saying *go on* in his voice and reaching for bread with fingers that do not work. He was given the whole of his answer while he was still able to follow it. He followed it. He said it out loud. And then he had no reason left to be saved, and so he was not.

Nobody kills him. Everyone present does the correct thing. Sevi says four words to him all afternoon and none of them is an argument.

### What the surrender costs

**Sevi's hands.** She does not set again, and she is not punished into it — nothing in her hands is damaged and everyone tells her so. She stops because of what she learned about herself on a wet flagstone with the deepest ground in the country coming up into her palm and sixty people on a bench inside easy reach, and how long the wanting lasted, and how good it felt. She is fifty-one and she was the best in the west of the country and everybody had been waiting to see what she would be at sixty. She dyes. The parish thinks it is grief and is kind about it, which she finds harder than the other thing.

**Neisa Vettamer.** She can still mend. Mending was done for two hundred years before the water was joined and it will be done for two hundred after, and all it takes is a hand. She has decided she will not ask for one, ever, and so forty times a year she tells somebody in a kitchen that his arm is going to be crooked and that she can make it straighter but she cannot make it right, in a flat voice, while a woman who would sit for a coin stands in the same room and both of them know it. She is twenty-six. She has fifty years of that in front of her.

**The ground.** They spend the following year taking the joining apart, which is worse work than building it: Talu lifting the floor of her own mile with a bar, Varo pulling channel-beds at nine crossings, Anka cutting the leats she spent thirty years defending. It is not one afternoon and one bar. It goes on through three springs, with the fever running the whole time, and every spring they have to choose it again. Two hundred years of held stock goes off down the fall. The low ground west-by-north is ordinary chalk now and comes back at the speed of grass, and nobody now alive will kneel on it and feel a draught.

**Hlemi Rannmer and the eight thousand at Nateska.** The pipe from Varenuor is broken at the head, because a pipe is a joining. They are on white downs with no spring inside the ground they were given, because that was arranged for them ninety years ago by people who told them the chalk was sound to build on, and it was. They haul water now, up a hill, twice a day, forever. Hlemi is fourteen with a forearm that was never mended and never will be, and he was not asked and there was nobody to ask him. Later he says: "Everybody carries water. I carry less than most because of the arm. They keep telling me I ought to be angry and I can't get round to it."

**Sootaavi.** With the ditch floors out, the drains slow and the hollows begin to stand water. It takes nine years and then the great hollow is reed again and the dairies that have not had a bad summer in a century shut, one at a time. Those families go on the road, and being on the road they are named for the road, and it is an insult that will outlive their grandchildren.

### What it does not fix

**The dead.** Vensa Kestamer is dead and nothing came back and nothing was ever going to. The forty at Hlemeska are dead with good bread in front of them. The nine at Nateska are still a fever. The woman on the bench beside Halu when he was nineteen, who came three times and died in the spring of nothing anybody could name, is still not spoken of, and cutting the water apart does not speak of her, and no one can be named who did it. Halu's death names nobody either. He is one man on a bridge. The grandchildren are still two inches shorter than the grandparents.

**The politics.** Nobody is punished and nothing is confessed. The thirty who understood the whole of it are alive, were mostly right about the good half, and are now the people who have to run a poorer country with less; several of them are the best people in Vessin. The parishes go on loving Halu. Inside a year the story is that a dyer knelt down at Varenuor and killed a sick old man who was trying to bring a woman back in daylight, and there is no way to prove otherwise, because the evidence was drains.

**The bench comes back, and there is more of it.** This is the worst thing and it is immediate. With the water cut apart every working is short again, and a short working wants a hand. So there are hands at more hearths more often than before — the courtesies intact for about a decade, bread first, meat afterwards, and then a coin instead. The ones who come back most often are still the ones who need the loaf. Halu's account of his country survives him completely intact and is more true after him than it was when he made it.

**Hollow work returns the same season.** A generation of masons has never once seen a joint dust out and believes that is because masons are better now. The first hard frost takes walls down at Hlemeska and in eleven parishes at once, because every joint in the country is the same age. There is water bread in three districts that winter. The poorest villages mend everything twice, which makes their material worthless, which makes the next mending worse. And the long bridge at Varenuor goes in the February after, all its joints together, having been hollow since the day it was closed and having had three hundred people a week on it for eleven years, and three parishes standing on it in August because a dyer needed them off the wet.

**The fever.** Standing water comes back to the hollows and after it comes the water nobody in Vessin has drunk in living memory. Anka counted it out loud before any of it happened and she was not wrong: it kills more people in the first six years than the bench killed in sixty, and the ones it kills are small children and old women, and nobody sat down to it and nobody was asked.

**The four hours.** What stood up in that trough went on taking off every living thing near it from the quarter hour until two o'clock, with a parish on a bridge and a crowd in the grass and nobody far enough off to be out of it. They all went home a meal behind and slept twelve hours and thought it was the heat. Nobody has told them and nobody is going to.

### The last page

Third day of a red. Sevi has the madder in from Tuesday and the bath has been under a boil since dawn, and it is the colour of weak tea going slowly to something better, which is what it did when her grandmother was a girl and what it will do now for as long as anyone is dyeing.

The girl she has taken on brings wood in and stacks it under the bench, and stands watching the bath the way apprentices do, waiting for something to happen quickly. Nothing happens quickly. Sevi shows her how to lift a corner of the cloth on the paddle and look at it against the light of the door, and how you never judge wet cloth for the colour it will be dry, and how you go on stirring anyway.

The girl has good hands. Sevi has seen it, and has not said so, and does not intend to.

The girl asks how you know when a bath is ready. Sevi tells her: you look, and you go on looking for three days, and one afternoon it is ready and you will not be able to say what changed.

They lift the cloth out together, both of them on the paddle, because it is heavy wet and Sevi's shoulder is not what it was. It goes across the trestle to drip and then over the line in the yard.

The stone out of the trough-bed at Varenuor is by the step where the cart left it, a good square block, dry-jointed on three faces. Sevi steps up on it to reach the line, pegs the cloth along, and gets down.

## Part Eight - The Spine in Play

Fourteen months, one frost to one August. The player is Sevi Kestamer's hands.

**Act One — The shed, the byre, the winter.** Prologue: Sevi at fifteen in her grandmother's shed at Kelaavi. Three workings, no dialogue about any of it. A full crock, a good bath, cloth ready, a dry still floor under the palm — the red does not come and the crock is untouched. Two valleys over in a wet hollow, same crock, same hands, the floor draughts cool and the red comes up: the player learns with her feet that the ground and the material are two different things. Back at Kelaavi in spring with a full floor and an empty crock and a customer at the door, she sets anyway; it rises eager and fast and better than she hoped, and the cloth is beautiful on the line and a dishcloth by morning. Then a small shortfall in a shut warm kitchen with a wet floor, and the dog comes in, and the working feels better than any she has done, and the dog is old the next week.

Thirty-six years later, the same hands. **What she can now do:** dye, mend small, read a floor with a flat palm, ask a wall who closed it. She carries dye to Hlemeska on the second frost and is in the byre when the plough-horse comes back without the boy. She is asked to sit as the hand and says she gave at haytime, so Liva of the Kelaavi road is fetched instead. The player holds the lamp. The small atrocity happens correctly, in front of her, with her name in it. Nobody falls in this act. What lands: Halu Aivemer is a name people say warmly, the man who ended the bench at Sootaavi, whose children are taller than the next parish's.

**Act Two — The dry spring.** Rain fails. Parishes are rested out of turn. **New:** travel between parishes and compare floors, which teaches by hand that the pot is full everywhere now, including where it has no business being. Svela of the Varenuor road appears — a setter at Kelaavi is found fed, walked home, and told the whole truth, and will not repeat it. The player hunts a woman who is honest with her victims and leaves them better informed than she found them. **Falls:** Ilma, made of his manners, who clears a room to work safely and is the last loose thing in it. **The Sootaavi Cut flips:** the player kneels in the dry bottom in June and finds her own vat-pattern at eighty times the size, running out of sight both ways, and a cool draught coming up in the third month of a drought.

**Act Three — The pipe.** **New:** the player can sit as a hand herself, and can refuse to be one, and both cost. She learns from the inside what twelve hours' sleep and two days' double eating buy. **Falls:** Riku, made of his hurry, who runs a district's whole season in one afternoon and takes sixty people out of the middle instead of sixty times off the top, and is stopped, and the season goes flat for nothing; and Hlusa, made of his courtesies, on a bench two days after she kept every one of them for a woman who had sat eleven times a year for six years and lost her teeth by spring anyway. **The Nateska downs flip:** Svela's trough, the wrist in the water where no working can be done, and the plain fact that the pipe puts eight thousand fed people at the loose end of every kitchen in Vessin. The player is told the reaching order is about looseness, not creatures, and can check it herself against the floor of her own shed.

**Act Four — The whole of it.** Somebody older finishes the player's sentence at a table one evening, and she says nothing for a long time. **New:** she can read the pattern in stone and lift it — Varo teaches her which courses come up with a bar and complains, as he has for thirty years, that this is why he has to relay them. **Falls:** Nemi, made of his grief, who could name every used-up person for three years in order and discovers she has been naming the same forty under different parishes, and is sent away, and takes two days to go quiet because Talu walks out and stays with her; and Sanu, made of his coldness, who has stood outside every pot for years and finds that cold does not put you outside the pot, it puts you last in it, and last still comes. **What lands:** Halu gets the player alone in a warm room and wins. He was a hand at nineteen, twice in one month, and he remembers the setter apologising and the woman on the bench beside him. She has no answer and the game gives her none.

**Act Five — August.** **Falls:** Svaali, made of the skill in his hands, taken as material in the fixed order, the hands last, because the last thing a pair of hands is wrong about is itself; then Toma, made of his belief that a harm you can see is a harm somebody can say no to, measured to death with his own instrument by a parish that counts the watchers, and the watchers' children, and then says out loud where the water goes.

Then the bridge. **New, and this is the whole of the act:** for the first time the player can pull hard and fast. The input exists. It works. Kneeling on the wet flags with her palm flat, she can feel the deepest ground in the country coming up into her hand and the sixty on the rail inside easy reach, and taking a working off a man whose joints have set is not difficult, only quick. The act is the length of time she keeps the palm flat. Everything else on that bridge is done by other people's ordinary competence: a hook along a rail, four stones and a bar, and three parishes stepping back onto dry ground because their mothers taught them to before they could talk.

## Part Nine - Rules of the Room

### The Law

**Nothing comes out of the pot that wasn't in the pot, and the fire's the ground's, not yours.**

Said flat, twice through. The trades have worn it into their own shapes and every shape says the same two things. No version of it has ever said whose pot it is, or where the pot ends.

### The three failure modes

**Flat** — the stock is short; the working stops before it does anything, nothing changes, and the stock is spent regardless. The lucky one.

**Hollow** — the stock is enough, the material is short, nothing near enough holds what is missing; the working finishes anyway with the shape held and the gap filled with whatever the pot has most of, and it reads sound for fifty years.

**Reaching** — the stock is enough, the material is short, and something within reach does hold what is wanted; the working goes and gets it, does not choose, and takes the loosest thing empty before it moves on.

### What is in the pot, and in what order it goes

A thing is in the pot if the steam off the working would reach it, and steam goes where the water goes. Touching is surest. Wet gives and dry holds back; warm gives and cold holds back; anything sharing water with the pot counts as touching, so a wet floor, a shared trough or a millrace puts things in the pot that are nowhere near it. Stone and cold draughts cut the reach short. A thing that shares no water with the pot is not in it at any distance.

The order is by how loose a thing is, and the list below is only the usual answer to that question: whatever is already in or touching the pot; then loose stores standing near — the flour in the sack, the salt in the crock, the water in the pail; then growing things; then animals; then people. Inside any body: **the newest meal, then the fat, then the water and the blood, then the teeth, then the muscle, then the bone.** It skips whatever has nothing in it of what it wants.

A dinner an hour old is looser than a hedge, a crop or a living sheep, and always was. That one sentence, held at the size of a country, is the whole of the apparatus.

### The loophole, in five sentences

The Law says nothing comes out of the pot that wasn't in it, and never said where the pot ends. Anything the steam would touch is in it, and a person is made of exactly what a loaf or a bone or a wall runs short of. You cannot use yourself, because the fire comes up through you and nothing goes back down a chimney. So when the pot is short you put somebody else's hand in the water, on purpose, before the working goes hunting the room for itself. You feed her first so it takes her dinner and not her blood, and you work slow so it comes off the top of her, and that is the whole of it.

### Pulling too hard

Pull harder than the ground under you will give and the difference comes out of the setter, in this order, and nothing reverses. First the taste goes — salt and nothing else, forever, which in a country of dairies, dye-houses, breweries and bake-ovens ends whatever else the man did for a living and leaves him with the setting that is eating him. Then the colour goes: hair, eyes and skin to the shade of unbleached cloth, no dye will hold on him, and no bruise will ever show on him again. Then he goes cold, sits by the fire in August, is cold to hold, cuts stay open for weeks, and his wife knows before he does. Last the skin thickens and the joints set a little more each year until he is bedbound and entirely sensible and cannot turn his head.

The land goes the same way and can be read from a cart. First the dew stops and the farmers argue about the weather for a year. Then the wells run clear and taste of nothing, and bread will not rise on that water and dye will not take in it, which is how a dye-house village finds out first. Then the grass, leaves and the coats of living animals go the colour of dry straw, in a tongue that follows the seep downhill and not the working. Last is white ground: not burnt, just finished, where nothing grows and nothing rots and a dead ewe dries out whole.

A man with nothing left to give at one stage does not stop there. He falls to the next.

### What setting on the dead produces

A risen, inside a quarter of an hour: warm, breathing, sitting up, and never the person. A body holds nearly everything a person is made of and is short of exactly the quiet work living flesh does all day, which is not a substance and cannot be brought or stood near, so the working never goes flat and never goes hollow. It reaches, every time, for the same thing, and finds it in whoever is standing there. What stands up is thin slices off the living, loosest first: their warmth, then their last few days, then the newest things in their heads, then the skill in their hands. It moves like the sister who was holding the hand, uses the mother's turns of phrase, and cannot say its own name. It goes on taking for as long as living things stay near it, and nothing is ever given back. Two gravediggers, never relatives, put a rope on it and walk it out at a rope's length onto bare or white ground, changing places at the near end. It goes quiet in an afternoon and cold by evening and is buried where it stopped. Nobody answers it on the way, and that is very hard, because it is using your mother's voice.

### The ten terms

**stock** — the life energy under the ground. **setting** — the work, and to do it; one who does it is a setter. **the pot** — everything a working counts as its source. **flat** — the clean failure. **hollow** — shape right, substance wrong. **reaching** — completion out of whatever is nearest and loosest. **the turn** — cold arriving sideways out of the room instead of up out of the floor. **a hand** — the person who puts a hand in the water on purpose. **a risen** — what stands up when someone sets on the dead. **white ground** — land used past coming back, pale and quiet, and therefore the safest ground there is.

Nothing else in this world gets a name of its own. If a thing needs saying, say it in the words a cook, a mason or a midwife would use.

### Things That Are Never True In This World

Nothing ever comes back, and no death in this story is ever undone, reversed, half-reversed, or softened by something that is nearly the person.

There is no deep well nobody has found, no old reserve, no vein of stock under a mountain: the ground has a level, the level is local, and the floor of it is never reached and never has to be, because the level is the only thing that matters.

Nobody is outside the pot — not the good, not the clever, not the protagonist, not the man who cut the warmth out of himself; cold and distance and dry stone buy you a place further down the order and the order always arrives.

Skill never stands in for material and stock never stands in for material: a full floor under an empty crock makes the failure louder and nothing else, and no character, however desperate or however loved, ever gets an exception to this.

The machine has no good use waiting to be found — no one ever discovers the right way to run it, and everything genuinely good it does (the pasture, the clean water, the bread that rises) is real, was always real, and is not payment for anything.

Nothing here is random and no rule bends once, for love or at the last minute: every horror in this story is a correct application of a rule an apprentice could be taught in a shed in an afternoon, and every one of them can be seen coming by anyone who was listening.
